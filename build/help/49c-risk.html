The Risk tab

The Risk tab works out the chance that someone in a family carries the family's variant, or will be affected, and the chance that a couple's child will be affected. Like a Bayesian risk calculation by hand, it takes account of everything known of the family, but it does so for the whole family at once, exactly, under the model of inheritance you choose. Below the answers, By hand has the four squares of a cross and a calculator that multiplies chances together, for working out the simpler cases yourself.

Work out the risk to a pregnancy takes you through three of the example families, step by step.

The family

From chooses the family: the drawing in the Studio tab, one of your files, or one of the examples. From my computer… adds a file from your computer to ~/pedigrees, and uses it. The Risk tab reads these files:

FileHow it is read
A drawingAs studio2ped reads it. Each entry in the colour key is a condition. A symbol filled with its colour, a quarter filled, or shaded all over, is affected; one half filled or half shaded is a carrier, and so is someone with a dot in their symbol. An empty symbol is unaffected, except for a pregnancy (P in the symbol) or a pregnancy loss, whose status isn't known yet. Identical twins have one genotype. A child joined to a couple by a dashed line is adopted, so that couple aren't the child's genetic parents, and a woman marked S is a surrogate, not a genetic mother.
A PED or MPED fileAs mpedtools reads it: 2 is affected, 1 unaffected and 0 not known, and in an MPED file, 3 is a carrier. Each column of an MPED file is a condition. If the file has several families, you choose one.
A GA4GH Pedigree or a Phenopackets FamilyIn Python, with pedigree-ga4gh, as PedNet reads it. Each condition of the people in it is a choice, and so is a Family's own record of who is affected, which may put several conditions together. A GA4GH Pedigree records who has a condition, but only sometimes who doesn't. People with no record of a condition are taken as unaffected if no one in the file is recorded as not having it, and as not known if anyone is. People with no record of the condition lets you choose for yourself.

The chances are for one condition at a time, which you choose under The condition. The line under it counts the people who are affected, who are marked as carriers and whose status isn't known. If something in the family had to be read in a particular way, such as an adoption, a note says so.

How it is inherited

  • Mode of inheritance: autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive or X-linked dominant. The chances depend on it, so if you aren't sure of the mode, compare the answers under each. PedNet's Mode of inheritance weighs up which mode the pattern of affected people suggests.
  • How common the variant is: the allele frequency, which is the share of the copies of the gene in the population that have the variant, or else how many people carry it. It matters most for the people who married into the family. When 1 in 25 people carry the variant, as for cystic fibrosis in northern Europe, an unaffected partner from the population is a carrier with a chance of 1 in 25. For a rare variant, it hardly changes the chances of the people born into the family.
  • Penetrance: the share of the people with the variant (with two copies, under a recessive mode) who are affected. Below 100%, someone unaffected may still have the variant.
  • Phenocopies and new mutations, which open under their own heading: the share of the people without the variant who are affected for another reason, and how often a new copy of the variant arises in an egg or a sperm. Both are 0 unless you give them.

Sometimes what is known of a family can't happen under the model you've chosen. For example, under a dominant mode with full penetrance and no new mutations, two unaffected parents can't have an affected child. The tab then says who can't be as they are, and what you could change.

The answers

One person
The person's chance of being a carrier, or of having the variant under a dominant mode or for a man under an X-linked one, and, if their own status isn't known, of being or becoming affected. Under these chances is the Bayesian table they come from. For each genotype, the table gives the prior chance, from the rest of the family; the conditional chance of what is known of the person and their descendants; the joint chance, which is the two multiplied together; and the posterior chance, which is the joint divided by the sum of the joints. How the chances are worked out explains it in full.
A child of a couple
The chance that a child of a couple who isn't born yet will be affected, and will be a carrier, given for a son and for a daughter under an X-linked mode. You can choose the parents of any child in the family, or a couple drawn as partners who have no children yet. If you choose someone in the family, and a partner from the population, the tab gives that person an unaffected partner who isn't related to them.
Everyone
Each person's chances, in a table. Click a name to see that person's chances and their Bayesian table above. With Test result, you can give someone a result, such as a carrier or doesn't have it, and see how it changes everyone's chances. The Risk tab takes a test result to be certain. You can also take someone's status as not known, which leaves out whether they are affected: do this for someone too young to show a condition that starts later in life. The results are used only in the Risk tab. They're kept in this browser for your next visit, but they aren't added to the family's file. Save as a table writes everyone's chances to a TSV file in ~/results, with a row for each person, which notebooks and spreadsheets can read.

By hand

By hand, at the bottom of the tab, shows what each child of a couple can inherit, drawn as chromosomes in a square of four equally likely children, for autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant and X-linked recessive inheritance. Under it, a calculator multiplies chances together into the chance that a baby is affected. For example, the child of the unaffected brother of someone with cystic fibrosis and a partner from the population has a chance of 2/3 × 1/25 × 1/4 = 1/150. Multiplying is right when each chance comes from a different part of the family. The rest of the tab works out every chance from the whole family at once, so it allows for what each person's relatives tell you about them as well.

From the other tabs

  • In the Studio tab, ⋯ (More) → Work out the risks in this family opens the drawing you're working on. When From is The drawing in the Studio tab, the Risk tab reads the drawing again each time you come back to it, so you can change the drawing and then see the new chances.
  • In the Files tab, Risks next to a drawing, a PED or MPED file or a GA4GH file opens it in the Risk tab. So does Risks next to a file that a tool has written, and Work out genetic risks on a file's page in the Tools tab.
What the chances leave out

The chances are exact for the model, but only as good as the model and what you know of the family. The model has one gene, with the same penetrance at every age and for men and women. It doesn't allow for a condition that more than one gene can cause, for linked markers, for mosaicism in a parent, or for a parent who isn't the genetic parent shown in the pedigree. Nor does it allow for age, although you can take the status of someone too young to show the condition as not known. A test that can miss a variant should be left out, or allowed for with a lower penetrance, because the tab takes every test result to be right. For inherited breast, ovarian and other cancers, use a model made for them, such as CanRisk, which allows for age and for several genes. Use the Risk tab to learn and to check your own sums, and for a real family, work with a clinical genetics service.
