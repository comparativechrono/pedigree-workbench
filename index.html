The Pedigree workbench

Draw a family in Pedigree Studio, then turn the drawing into the files that other people and other programs need. Linkage programs read PED files, and the GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets files let you share a family's pedigree and findings in the international standard. The tools that do this are our Python packages, which run here in your browser. Others analyse a family as a graph and weigh up how its condition is inherited and which variants co-segregate with it (PedNet), simulate families (PedGen), and read a pedigree from a picture or a sketch, into a PED file and a drawing (PedInterp). You can use them all by pointing and clicking in the Tools tab, by typing commands in the Terminal tab, or by writing code in the Notebook tab. The Risk tab works out, from a drawing or a file, the chance that each person in a family carries its variant, and that a couple's child will be affected.

The formats, and the tools between them

The drawing is in the middle. Each arrow is a tool that turns one kind of file into another. Click an arrow to open the tool, or click a box to see what you can do with that kind of file.

Everything stays on this computer

Pedigree Studio and Python run inside this web page. Your drawings and files are kept in this browser, on this computer, and aren't sent anywhere, with one exception. If you ask Match conditions to terms or Fill in missing terms to search EBI's Ontology Lookup Service, the names you search for, and any ids you type, go to EBI. Nothing else is sent, not even the drawing. Privacy has the details, and Your files explains how to keep copies.

A first session

Take ten minutes to see what the workbench is for, using one of the example families. Each step has a button that does it for you.

1 · Open a drawing

The Studio tab is Pedigree Studio. Open the Okafor family, one of the examples. It has four generations, with twin boys who have had hearing loss from birth and whose parents are cousins. The twins' grandmother has had breast cancer.

The bar above the drawing says which file the drawing belongs to. The examples can't be changed, so an example opens as a new drawing that hasn't been saved yet. Change it as much as you like, then use Save as… to keep your version in ~/pedigrees. The Studio's own toolbar and its ? button explain how to draw.

2 · Give its conditions ontology terms

The colour key names two conditions. A GA4GH file names conditions by ontology terms rather than in free text: diseases by the Mondo Disease Ontology, and signs and symptoms by the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO). The examples come with a terms file that already matches them. For a family of your own, make one with the Match conditions to terms tool (here's how).

3 · Convert it

The Tools tab has a form for each of our tools. Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets writes the family as a GA4GH Pedigree and a Phenopackets Family. The button below opens the tool with the drawing and the examples' terms file (/data/examples/example-terms.json) already chosen. Then press Run. The first run starts Python in your browser, which takes a few seconds. The very first time, it also downloads about 10 MB.

Under the form, The same in the terminal, and in Python shows exactly what the tool runs: a command for the Terminal tab and code for a notebook. You can use them to do the same job again without the form.

4 · Check the result

The files the tool writes appear under the result and are saved in ~/results. Press Check next to a file to check it against the standards. The check works in the same way as phenopacket-tools, the Phenopackets team's own validator, and it also tells you how to fix anything that's wrong.

5 · Back to a drawing, or on to a PED file

  • GA4GH or Phenopackets → drawing draws a GA4GH file in Pedigree Studio, whether you made it here or someone has sent it to you.
  • Drawing → PED or MPED writes the PED file that linkage programs read.

6 · Keep your work

Everything you make is kept in this browser, on this computer. To keep it anywhere else, download it with a file's Download button, or with My files → Download all my files (.zip) at the top right. Your files has the details.

Share a family in the GA4GH standard

This walkthrough takes you from a drawing to files you can send to a collaborator, a registry or another program: a GA4GH Pedigree and a Phenopackets Family. The buttons use the Okafor family, one of the examples. With a family of your own, choose your drawing instead.

Which of the two files to send

The Phenopackets Family follows Phenopackets version 2, an approved GA4GH standard, so you can send it to anything that reads Phenopackets. The GA4GH Pedigree holds the whole family, with every relationship named by a kinship term. It's in the form proposed for the GA4GH Pedigree Standard, which is still being written. If you're not sure what the recipient reads, send both. The GA4GH Pedigree explains the difference.

1 · The drawing

Draw the family in the Studio tab, or open the drawing you have. Every condition needs a colour, and a name in the colour key (the Studio's Key button). The name is how the tools know what the colour means. Save the drawing among your files with Save as…, because the tools work on saved files.

Whatever you write under the symbols, such as names, ages and notes, stays in the drawing. The files name people by their pedigree numbers (I-1, II-3, IV-3), unless you choose otherwise in step 3.

2 · Terms for the conditions

A GA4GH file names a condition by an ontology term: a disease by its Mondo term, and a sign or symptom by its HPO term. Match conditions to terms finds the terms for the conditions in the drawing's colour key, and keeps your choices in a terms file. For each condition, choose whether it's a disease or a sign, press Suggest and pick the right term. When you've done them all, press Save.

The examples come with a terms file, /data/examples/example-terms.json, that already has these terms, so you can skip this step with the Okafor family. Give the conditions ontology terms goes through it in more detail.

3 · Convert the drawing

Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets writes the two files. Choose the drawing and the terms file. Under the terms file, a table shows the term each condition will get. A condition without a term is left out of the files, so match it first. Then press Run.

It writes ~/results/okafor_ga4gh-pedigree.json and ~/results/okafor_phenopackets-family.json. They hold everyone in the drawing, with their sex, whether they're alive and their conditions as terms. They also hold the relationships between people, and say who the proband is. Some things the files have no field for, such as a pregnancy or a carrier, are noted in the GA4GH Pedigree's narrative. If something didn't go in as drawn, such as a symbol that stands for several people, the tool tells you under the result.

4 · Check the files

Press Check next to a file, or use Check against the standard in the Tools tab. It checks the file in the same way as phenopacket-tools, the Phenopackets team's own validator. For a GA4GH Pedigree, it checks the family as well. It should say Valid. Errors make a file invalid, and warnings are worth reading. Notes are for information, such as an ontology release that wasn't recorded. The example files were made by the tool in step 3, so you can try the check on them now:

5 · Look at what is in them

Look inside a GA4GH file shows a file as a table. It lists each person with their sex, status and terms, and then the relationships. It's the quickest way to make sure the files say what you meant.

6 · De-identify before the files leave you

De-identify gives everyone a new id (IND-0001, IND-0002 …) everywhere in the file, and takes out dates of birth and other ids. It can also write a key that matches the old ids to the new ones. Keep the key, and the drawing, separate from what you share.

What de-identifying does not do

It removes what the file says directly, but a family can still be recognised from what's left, such as a rare condition, the shape of a large family or people's ages. So whether a file is safe to share is a judgement for you, and for your institution's rules on sharing patient data.

7 · Download them

Each file under a result has a Download button. To take everything at once, use My files → Download all my files (.zip) at the top right.

The same in the terminal

Every step is a command, so you can do the whole job again in one go:

studio2ga4gh /data/examples/okafor.studio.json -t /data/examples/example-terms.json -d ~/results

phenotk view ~/results/okafor_ga4gh-pedigree.json
phenotk deidentify ~/results/okafor_phenopackets-family.json -o ~/results/okafor.deidentified.json --keyfile ~/results/okafor.key.json

Make a PED file for a linkage program

Linkage and association programs read pedigrees as PED files. A PED file has one line per person, giving their parents, their sex and whether they're affected. There are two ways to make one from a drawing, and they differ in how they deal with more than one condition and with carriers.

From the drawing

1 · Write the MPED file

Drawing → PED or MPED writes a PED file for a drawing with one condition. When the drawing has more, or shows carriers, it writes an MPED file, which is a PED file with a column for each condition.

It checks the file with mpedtools straight away, and tells you if anything is wrong.

2 · Check the ids

studio2ped names each person by their pedigree number, such as II-3, just as the GA4GH files do. This means you can share the file without anyone's name in it. With People are named by, you can choose the drawing's labels or Pedigree Studio's own ids (p-ggf) instead, or the text under the symbols, which is what version 0.1 used. In the Okafor family, that text gives Grace_78 and Emeka_d_70, which are names. If two people would get the same id, the pedigree number is used instead, with a note.

3 · One condition at a time

Most linkage programs read one condition at a time. Take one condition out makes a plain PED file for one column of the MPED file, or one for each column if you leave the condition empty. A plain PED file has no code for a carrier, so carriers become unaffected (1).

Through the GA4GH files

First write the GA4GH files, following steps 1 to 3 of Share a family in the GA4GH standard. Then GA4GH → PED, table or YAML makes the PED file, with the pedigree numbers as ids.

Make it from the Phenopackets Family, because its pedigree records who is unaffected (1) as well as who is affected (2). A GA4GH Pedigree records only the conditions people have, so a PED file made from it marks everyone else 0, unknown. In the Okafor family, affected means having either condition, so the grandmother with breast cancer and the twins with hearing loss are all affected. That isn't what you need for a linkage analysis of one of the conditions. For that, take the first route.

The same in the terminal

studio2ped /data/examples/okafor.studio.json -d ~/results -o okafor
mpedtools validate ~/results/okafor.mped
mpedtools extract ~/results/okafor.mped Hearing_loss_from_birth -o ~/results/okafor_hearing.ped
phenotk convert /data/examples/okafor_phenopackets-family.json --to ped -o ~/results/okafor-numbers.ped

The PED and MPED formats describes the columns and codes.

Draw a PED file you have been sent

It's hard to read a family from a PED file. Once the file is drawn in Pedigree Studio, you can check the family at a glance, add what the PED file leaves out, and save the drawing as a picture, or go on to convert it to the GA4GH files. The example is the PED file of the Lynch family, in which several people have had bowel or pancreatic cancer.

1 · Add the file

Add the PED file to your files with My files → Add files from my computer… at the top right. Or use From my computer… under the file box of the tool in the next step. This puts the file in ~/data. The workbench recognises a PED file by its ending, .ped or .fam (PLINK's name for the same six columns), and an MPED file by .mped. If your file ends in something else, rename it.

2 · Draw it

PED or MPED → drawing lays the family out generation by generation and fills in the affected people (status 2). Choose the colour, and whether to fill the whole symbol or the left half, or to shade with a pattern. An MPED file's conditions each get a colour of their own.

The drawing is saved in ~/pedigrees. To see it, press Open the drawing in Studio under the result. If Tidy the drawing when it opens was ticked on the form, Pedigree Studio's Tidy lays the drawing out again as it opens, which usually gives the neatest result.

3 · Finish it in the Studio

A PED file gives only each person's parents, sex and affected status, so that's all the drawing has. In the Studio, you can add the rest:

  • The condition's name. The colour key calls it Affected, or gives it its column name if the drawing came from an MPED file. Click the key and type the condition's name, so that the drawing can be matched to terms and converted.
  • Who has died, twins, adoptions, pregnancies and the proband. To add these, select a person or a line and use the panel.
  • Labels. Each person is labelled with their id from the PED file. You can add ages or notes under it if you like.

Then press Save in the bar above the drawing. Pedigree Studio's File menu exports a picture (PNG or SVG) to your computer.

Draw a GA4GH or Phenopackets file

This walkthrough shows you how to draw a GA4GH Pedigree or a Phenopackets Family in Pedigree Studio, with each condition in the colour key under its term. You can change the drawing and convert it back, and the terms come back with it.

1 · Add the file

Add it with My files → Add files from my computer…, or with From my computer… under the file box of the tool you use next. The workbench tells GA4GH files apart by what's in them, whatever they're called. If it shows a file as plain JSON, it hasn't recognised it as a GA4GH file, but Upgrade a file from an older tool may still be able to read it.

2 · If it was made by an older tool, upgrade it

Files made by studio2ga4gh 0.3 or abusua2ga4gh 0.2 aren't in the standard form, and the tools refuse them with a message explaining this. Upgrade a file from an older tool rewrites such a file in the standard form. You only need to do this once for each file. It lists what it changed, and anything it couldn't do.

The older files often name conditions without a term. The upgrade lists them, and Fill in missing terms gives them their terms from a terms file (here's how). The drawing doesn't need the terms, because a condition without one is drawn with its name, but the GA4GH files do. A GA4GH Pedigree from studio2ga4gh 0.3 recorded only whether people were affected, not what they were affected by. Draw from the Phenopackets file made with it instead, because that has the conditions.

To find out whether a file needs upgrading, open its summary in the Tools tab with the More… button next to the file. The summary says Older form if the file needs upgrading.

3 · Draw it

GA4GH or Phenopackets → drawing lays the family out from its relationships. It gives each condition a colour, and names it in the colour key with its term, such as breast cancer [MONDO:0007254].

Under the result, Open the drawing in Studio shows it. If Tidy the drawing when it opens was ticked, Pedigree Studio's Tidy lays the drawing out again as it opens.

4 · Check the drawing

How much of the family comes back depends on the file:

  • From a GA4GH Pedigree: everyone, the couples (including consanguineous and separated ones), twins, adoptions, who has died, and the pregnancies and losses that its narrative records.
  • From a Phenopackets Family: everyone in its pedigree, with their parents, sex and conditions. A Family's pedigree is a PED file in another form, so it has no twins and no adoptions. Draw from the GA4GH Pedigree when you have one.

People are labelled with their ids, except where the ids are pedigree numbers such as II-3, because Pedigree Studio numbers the generations itself. Pedigree Studio draws children from couples, so if only one of a child's parents is in the file, the child is given an unknown parent as the other.

The same in the terminal

pedigree-ga4gh upgrade /data/examples/older/studio2ga4gh-0.3_phenopackets-family.json -o ~/results/upgraded_phenopackets-family.json
phenotk normalise ~/results/upgraded_phenopackets-family.json --mapping /data/examples/example-terms.json --no-online -o ~/results/upgraded_phenopackets-family.json
pedigree-ga4gh validate ~/results/upgraded_phenopackets-family.json
ga4gh2studio ~/results/upgraded_phenopackets-family.json -o ~/pedigrees/upgraded.studio.json
studio --tidy ~/pedigrees/upgraded.studio.json

Check a GA4GH file, or update an old one

The check reads a GA4GH Pedigree, a Phenopackets Family or Cohort, or a single Phenopacket. It tells you whether the file is correct, and how to fix anything that isn't. Use it on files you make before you send them, and on files you're sent before you rely on them.

1 · Check the file

Check against the standard takes one file. The answer comes first – Valid or Not valid – followed by a table with a line for each finding. Each line says what's wrong, where it is in the file (a path such as individuals[4].metaData) and how to fix it.

  • Errors make the file invalid, so other software may refuse it or read it wrongly.
  • Warnings are about things that are allowed but probably mistaken, such as a mother recorded as male.
  • Notes are for information, such as an ontology release that the file doesn't record.

The checks are those of the Phenopackets version 2 schema and of phenopacket-tools, the Phenopackets team's own validator. On top of these come the GA4GH Pedigree's own checks: KIN terms, people named in relationships, parents' sexes and loops of ancestry. Check against the standard lists them.

3 · Upgrade an older file

Upgrade a file from an older tool rewrites the file in the standard form. It lists every change, and anything it couldn't do, which usually means conditions without a term. Under the result, Check the upgraded file checks it again.

Among the things it corrects in files from abusua2ga4gh 0.2 are names inside subject, which the standard has no place for, and a sperm donor written as KIN:039, which in KIN means hasGestationalCarrier. It also corrects carriers given the HPO term HP:0032500, which is Exacerbated by tobacco use. Anything it can't tell from the file, it leaves alone and tells you about. For example, abusua2ga4gh wrote foster and social parents as adoptive parents, and only the free-text note, which the upgrade keeps in the narrative, says which they were. The GA4GH Pedigree lists what was wrong with the older files, and why.

Give the conditions ontology terms

GA4GH and Phenopackets files name each condition by a term from an ontology, so that software and people everywhere read it in the same way. A disease is named by a term from the Mondo Disease Ontology, and a sign or symptom by a term from the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO). This walkthrough shows you how to find the terms for a drawing's conditions, and how to fill in the terms that an existing file lacks.

For a drawing: Match conditions to terms

1 · Choose the drawing

The tool lists the conditions in the drawing's colour key, with the number of people who have each one. If you keep a terms file for a project, start from it. Conditions it already has are filled in, and your new matches are added to it.

2 · Choose where to search

  • EBI's Ontology Lookup Service searches the latest releases, and finds terms that a local search can miss. It needs the internet, and it sends EBI the words you search for and any ids you type, but never the drawing.
  • Copies of the ontologies on this computer work without the internet and send nothing. The tool can download the HPO (about 11 MB) into ~/ontologies for you. Mondo's file is about 50 MB, and its host doesn't let web pages download it. Instead, download mondo.obo with your browser from Mondo's download page, then add it with From my computer…. The first search of a file reads all of it, which takes several seconds.

3 · Match each condition

For each condition, the tool guesses from the name whether it's a disease (Mondo) or a sign or symptom (HPO). Change its choice if it's wrong, then press Suggest. Each suggestion shows the term's id and label, whether its label or a synonym matched, a score and the term's definition. Choose the one that's right. If none is, change the words and search again. If you already know the id, type it and press Use this id. The tool looks it up, so that its label and release are recorded.

Choosing a term

Choose the most specific term that's true of everyone with the condition in this family. Hearing impairment (HP:0000365) is true of the Okafor twins. If their hearing loss is known to be sensorineural, Congenital sensorineural hearing impairment (HP:0008527) says more. Use a Mondo term when there's a diagnosis, and HPO terms for features without one. Read the definition, because two terms can share a name, as breast cancer (MONDO:0007254) and breast carcinoma (MONDO:0004989) nearly do.

4 · Keep the matches

Save writes the terms file, which for the Price family is ~/terms/price-terms.json. Tick Also write the ids into the drawing's colour key to make a copy of the drawing whose key reads Breast cancer [MONDO:0007254]. A drawing like that carries its terms wherever it goes, and converts without a terms file. Then press Next to open Drawing → GA4GH with the drawing and the terms file already chosen.

For a GA4GH file: Fill in missing terms

Files from older tools, or from elsewhere, may name conditions without their ids. Fill in missing terms gives each one its id from a terms file, ignoring capital letters when it matches the names. It can also look up names that aren't in the terms file. It uses a term it finds when the term's name, or one of its synonyms, is the same as the condition's name, ignoring capital letters and punctuation. With a lower threshold it takes close matches too, but each of those is a guess for you to check. It writes a log of what it decided next to the new file, ….normalised.log.json.

A file made by an older tool must be upgraded first, and the tool tells you when that's needed. To try this, upgrade the older example, then fill in its terms from the examples' terms file. The tool picks your newest file, which is the upgraded one.

A terms file

A terms file is a small JSON file. It lists each condition's name in the drawing, with its term and the release of the ontology the term came from. You can also write one by hand, giving just the id if you like.

{
  "Breast cancer": {"id": "MONDO:0007254", "label": "breast cancer", "version": "2026-10-06"},
  "Hearing loss from birth": {"id": "HP:0000365", "label": "Hearing impairment", "version": "2026-09-01"},
  "Ovarian cancer": "MONDO:0008170"
}

One terms file can serve many drawings, and capital letters don't matter when the names are matched. Ontology terms and terms files has more.

hponorm and mondonorm, the programs behind the searches, match the names in a GA4GH or Phenopackets file by asking you about each one as they go. This terminal can't pass your answers to them, so here they stop at the first question. They can take their answers from a file instead: hponorm FILE.json --mapping answers.json --auto-remembered -o OUT.json. In Python, their searches look like this:

Weigh up the mode of inheritance, and rank candidate variants

From a family's PED file, PedNet shows how the family is put together, weighs up how its condition is inherited, and ranks the variants found by sequencing some of its members by how well each co-segregates with the condition. This walkthrough uses three of the example families: the Okafor family, whose twin boys with hearing loss are the sons of first cousins; the Lynch family, with bowel cancer over three generations; and one of PedNet's own families, with six candidate variants.

The tools read PED files, and GA4GH Pedigrees and Phenopackets Families too. To use one of your drawings, make it into one of these first, with Drawing → PED or MPED or Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets.

1 · The family as a graph

The pedigree as a graph lists the family's loops, who is inbred, and how closely the affected people are related. In the Okafor family, the twins' parents, III-2 and III-3, are first cousins: III-2's father and III-3's mother, II-2 and II-3, are brother and sister, so the marriage closes a loop through them. All five of the couple's children have an inbreeding coefficient of 1/16.

From an MPED file, PedNet takes the first condition: for the Okafor family, hearing loss from birth.

2 · The mode of inheritance

Mode of inheritance gives the probability of each mode. In the Lynch family, there is cancer in three generations in a row: in I-1, in his sons II-2 and II-3, and in II-2's daughters III-2 and III-3. Autosomal dominant inheritance has a probability of 0.97.

In the Okafor family, two affected brothers whose parents are cousins make a recessive condition almost certain, at 0.98, but they can't show whether it is autosomal or X-linked. The answer is uncertain, with autosomal recessive at 0.69 and X-linked recessive at 0.29. That is all one family can say. If other families have the same condition, put them in one PED file, and tick The families have the same condition.

3 · Rank the candidate variants

Rank candidate variants needs the PED file and the variants: a VCF file whose samples are named with the PED file's ids, or a table of genotypes. PedNet's example family has a dominant condition, and six variants found in some of its members.

var1, which all six of the affected people carry and none of the three unaffected people who were tested, comes first, with a log10 Bayes factor of 2.05: strong evidence for co-segregation (PP1_Strong). Each of the others is strong evidence against causing the condition (BS4_Strong). The variants are ranked by their odds of being the cause, which take account of whether a variant's chromosome suits the family's mode of inheritance as well as of its Bayes factor. This family's condition is almost certainly autosomal, so var5, on the X chromosome, comes fourth, below var2 and var4, though its Bayes factor is higher than theirs.

PedNet also notes genotypes that break Mendel's laws. Only III-1 carries var6, though neither of his parents does, which a new mutation in III-1 would explain. var5 breaks them three times: II-3 and III-2 don't carry it, though their fathers do, and a father passes his X chromosome to every daughter; and III-1 carries it, though his mother doesn't. A new mutation in III-1 would explain only the last of these.

The tool saves every score in a table, ~/results/dominant-ranked.tsv, which you can open in the Files tab or a spreadsheet.

What the evidence means

Co-segregation is one line of evidence among many under the ACMG/AMP guidelines, and PP1 applies only to a gene that is known to cause the condition. The Bayes factor assumes that the PED file is right and that the genotypes were called correctly, apart from a small error rate. A variant that co-segregates may only be close to the one that causes the condition. Weigh it with the rest of the evidence.

The same in the terminal

pednet mode /data/examples/lynch.ped
pednet mode /data/examples/okafor.mped
pednet rank /data/examples/pednet/dominant.ped /data/examples/pednet/dominant.vcf --out ~/results/dominant-ranked.tsv

The notebook ~/notebooks/pedigree-analysis.ipynb does the same in Python, and goes on to simulated families and pictures.

Work out the risk to a pregnancy

The Risk tab works out the chance that a pregnancy, or any child of a couple, will be affected, from everything known of the whole family. This walkthrough uses three of the example families: a couple who are second cousins, with cystic fibrosis in the family; the Lynch family, with bowel cancer over three generations; and the Okafor family, whose twin boys have had hearing loss from birth.

1 · A couple with cystic fibrosis in the family

Lisa (IV-1) and David (IV-2) are second cousins, and Lisa is pregnant. David's sister Amelia has cystic fibrosis, an autosomal recessive condition, and Lisa's father Michael is known to be a carrier, which is what the dot in his symbol means. The button opens the family with the numbers for cystic fibrosis in northern Europe, where 1 in 25 people carry a variant.

The pregnancy (V-1) will be affected with a chance of about 1 in 12 (8.4%). Under Everyone, click David's name. As the unaffected brother of someone affected, he is a carrier with a chance of 2/3: his Bayesian table shows the 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4 of his parents' cross, and the two copies ruled out because he is unaffected. Lisa is a carrier with a chance of about 51%. That is 1/2 from her father, and a little more, because her mother Anne, who isn't related to the family, could be a carrier too.

By hand, the pregnancy's chance is the chance that David is a carrier, times the chance that Lisa is, times 1/4: 2/3 × 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/12.

The Risk tab's chance is a little higher because it allows for Anne. To see this, find Anne (III-2) under Everyone and give her the test result doesn't have it. Lisa's chance becomes 1 in 2, and the pregnancy's exactly 1 in 12. Set the result back to — when you're done.

2 · A Bayesian table, with reduced penetrance

In the Lynch family, bowel cancer has been passed down three generations. Ann (III-4), who is 48, has had no cancer, but her father had. Take the condition to be autosomal dominant, with a penetrance of 80%.

As the daughter of an affected father, Ann had a chance of 1/2 of inheriting the variant: that is her prior. Her Bayesian table takes account of what is known of her and her daughters, IV-3 and IV-4, who are unaffected too. If Ann has the variant, the chance that all three of them are unaffected is 0.2 × 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.072, or about 1 in 14. Ann would have escaped the cancer, with a chance of 0.2, and each daughter would have either not inherited the variant or escaped the cancer, with a chance of 1/2 + 1/2 × 0.2 = 0.6. If Ann doesn't have the variant, the chance is nearly 1. These are the conditionals, and her posterior chance of having the variant is about 1 in 15 (6.7%). Her cousin Gary (III-6) has no children, so only the fact that he is unaffected counts, and his chance is about 1 in 6, as for any unaffected child of an affected parent at this penetrance.

The Risk tab doesn't allow for age, though, and bowel cancer usually starts later in life, so the fact that Ann's daughters, at 21 and 26, haven't had it says little. Under Everyone, set Test result or status for IV-3 and IV-4 to not known (leave it out). Ann's chance rises to about 1 in 6, the same as Gary's.

3 · The mode of inheritance matters

In the Okafor family, the twins Chidi and Dubem (IV-3 and IV-4) have had hearing loss from birth, and their parents, Obi and Amaka (III-2 and III-3), are first cousins. Amaka is pregnant again (IV-5). If the hearing loss is autosomal recessive, Obi and Amaka must both be carriers, and the pregnancy will be affected with a chance of 1 in 4.

If it is X-linked recessive instead, Amaka must be a carrier, and a son's X chromosome comes from her alone. Under A child of a couple, a son of theirs has a chance of 1/2 of being affected, and a daughter none, as Obi is unaffected. Before its sex is known, the pregnancy's chance is 1 in 4 again, but for a boy it is 1/2. An affected pair of brothers can't tell these two modes apart: PedNet puts autosomal recessive at 0.69 and X-linked recessive at 0.29. The Risk tab can't choose the mode for you, but it shows you what each mode would mean for the family.

4 · Your own family

Draw the family in the Studio tab, with the condition in the colour key, then choose ⋯ (More) → Work out the risks in this family. Or choose a drawing, a PED file or a GA4GH file under From, or use Risks next to a file in the Files tab. The Risk tab reads the drawing in the Studio tab again each time you come back to it, so you can go back and forth between the two: mark someone as affected or as a carrier in the Studio, then see how the chances change.

Save as a table writes everyone's chances to ~/results, as a TSV file with a row for each person. In a notebook, pandas reads it: pd.read_csv("/home/user/results/cf-cousins_risks.tsv", sep="\t").

The Risk tab describes everything on the tab, and How the chances are worked out explains the sums.

Pedigree Studio's own help, under its ? button, explains how to draw, covering the symbols, the toolbar and the panels. In short:

  1. Add the first person with Male, Female or Unknown on the toolbar. This is usually the person the pedigree is for, and the one you mark as the proband in the panel.
  2. Build out from them with Add a relative. With someone selected, it adds a partner, a child, parents, or a brother or sister, already joined up.
  3. Join people yourself with Partners, Child and Twins.
  4. Describe each person in the panel. You can add notes under the symbol, mark someone as deceased and add shading for a condition. You can also put a mark inside the symbol for a carrier, a pregnancy, a donor or a surrogate.
  5. Click a line to mark a couple as consanguineous or separated, or to make a line of descent dashed to show an adoption.
  6. Tidy lays it all out again.

What the tools read from a drawing

  • The colour key names the conditions. Give each condition its own colour (or a half, a quarter or a pattern), and name it in the key with the Key button. A colour that the key doesn't name isn't counted as a condition. An id in brackets after the name, as in Breast cancer [MONDO:0007254], gives the condition its term (Ontology terms and terms files). A long name widens the key to fit it on one line, unless you choose Wrap the text under Long entries in the key in the Studio's File menu; then drag the key's right edge to set its width.
  • The proband becomes the index patient in the GA4GH files.
  • The notes under the symbols stay in the drawing. The GA4GH and PED files name people by their pedigree numbers, unless you choose otherwise.
  • Carriers, pregnancies, losses, donors and surrogates are written in the ways the standard allows. Losses and pregnancies are written as people, and donors and surrogates by their KIN relationships. Anything that has no place of its own, such as a carrier, goes in the GA4GH Pedigree's narrative.
  • A symbol that stands for several people, shown by a number inside it, is written as one person, and the tool tells you so under the result.

A workflow chains the tools of the Tools tab together, so that the files one step writes go straight into the next. You choose its input files and press Run, and the steps run one after another, exactly as they would if you ran each tool yourself. Each run keeps its files in a folder of its own, with a record of how they were made. If you've used workflows in Galaxy, these will look familiar.

The Workflows page

Open it with Workflows, next to All tools at the top of the Tools tab. It lists:

  • Your workflows, the ones you've made or copied, each a file in ~/workflows, with buttons to run, edit, download or delete it.
  • Examples, ready to run. Edit a copy makes one yours to change.
  • Runs, the newest first. View shows what a run did, step by step, and Folder opens its folder in the Files tab.

At the top, New workflow opens the editor on an empty workflow, Make one from the tools you've run turns tools you've run into a workflow, and Add a workflow file… adds one from your computer, such as one someone has sent you.

Running a workflow

Run opens a form with a box for each of the workflow's input files, numbered as they are in the editor. Each starts with the file you gave it last time, or else the one the workflow suggests, or else the first file that fits, from your own files or the examples. An optional input, such as a terms file, can be left as none. Under the inputs:

  • Put the results in is the run's folder. It is named after the workflow and the first input file, such as ~/results/share-a-drawing-in-the-ga4gh-standard/okafor, with -2, -3 and so on added if that folder is taken. You can choose another, anywhere in your home folder.
  • The steps lists the tools in the order they run, with any settings of their own.

Press Run, and each step shows waiting, running, then done or failed, with a line on what it did: the files it wrote, or what a check found. Python starts the first time, as it does for a tool. You can go to another tab while the steps run, and a message tells you when they have finished. Stop stops the run after the step that is running. Only one workflow runs at a time.

If a step fails, the steps that read its files don't run, and the others still do. What went wrong, under a step that failed, says why, with what the program printed. Under a step that worked, What it did has what the program printed, the files it wrote, with the same buttons as under a tool's result, and its command. When the run has finished, you can open its folder in the Files tab, open its steps as a notebook, run it again (on another file, perhaps), or edit the workflow.

What each run keeps

  • The files its steps write, named as the tools name them. The GA4GH Pedigree made from okafor.studio.json is okafor_ga4gh-pedigree.json, just as it is when you run the tool yourself. A tool that writes a set of files, such as One file for each person, puts them in a folder inside the run's folder. If a step would write a file with the same name as one an earlier step wrote, its files go in a folder of their own, named after the step, such as 4-fill-in-missing-terms.
  • A record of the run, workflow-run.json. It holds the workflow as it was, the input files, and for each step its settings, its command, what it printed and the files it wrote. Open it in the Files tab to see the run again.
  • Its commands, commands.sh. bash commands.sh in the Terminal tab runs the steps again. On your own computer, with the packages installed, it does the same, once any paths that begin /data/examples point to your copies of the examples.

Open the steps as a notebook writes a notebook in ~/notebooks with each step's Python, as the Tools tab shows it under each tool, and opens it in the Notebook tab.

The editor

New workflow, Edit on one of yours and Edit a copy on an example all open the editor, which takes up the whole width of the Tools tab. Each input file and each step is a box, numbered in the order they run. A step's input files are on the left of its box, and the files it writes are on the right, each with a dot.

  • + Input file adds a file the workflow is given when it runs. Click its box to name it, to say what kind of file it is, and to choose a file to suggest. Tick Optional for a file that the steps can do without, such as a terms file.
  • + Add a step adds a tool. If a box is selected, the new step goes to its right, and reads one of its files if it can.
  • The + by a file lists the tools that read that kind of file. The step you choose reads it.
  • Drag from a dot on the right of a box to an input on the left of another to connect them. While you drag, the inputs that can take the file grow and turn green, and the others fade: a file can only go into an input that takes its kind. Click a line to take that connection away.
  • Click a box for its settings, in the column beside the boxes (below them on a narrow screen). For a step, you choose where each of its input files comes from, or press Make it an input of the workflow, and you set the tool's options, as in its form. Where its files go isn't one of them, because each run decides that. A setting that the tool works out from the name of a file, such as PedInterp's family id, is worked out when the workflow runs, unless you fill it in. A choice that a tool makes from the kind of file it's given, such as what GA4GH Pedigree ⇄ Phenopackets Family makes, is made here instead, so that the step always writes the kind of file its box shows.
  • Click the background for the workflow's description and its Checks, which list what stops it running, such as an input that isn't connected. Click a check to go to its step. When nothing does, it says Ready to run.
  • Tidy arranges the boxes in columns, with the inputs on the left. To move a box, drag it. On a touch screen, drag it by its title.

Your changes are saved as you go, and Saved, beside the name, tells you when they have been. Change the name at the top left, and the workflow's file is renamed to match. The download button, beside Run, saves the file, NAME.workflow.json, to your computer, to keep or to send to someone. They add it with Add a workflow file…, which puts it in their ~/workflows under a name of its own, so that a workflow of theirs with the same name is kept.

A workflow from the tools you've run

Make one from the tools you've run does what Galaxy's Extract workflow does. Run the tools in the Tools tab one after another, each on a file the one before wrote, then come to this page. Tick the tools to use (the ones that worked in the last three hours are ticked already), give the workflow a name, and press Make the workflow. A file that one of them wrote and a later one read becomes a connection between their steps. Any other file they read becomes an input of the workflow, which suggests the file you used, and the settings you chose are kept. The page lists the last 40 tools you ran.

What can be a step

  • Every tool of the Tools tab, except Match conditions to terms, which asks you questions as it goes. Make the terms file with it first, then give the file to the workflow as an input, as the example Share a drawing in the GA4GH standard does.
  • The checks, and the tools that only report, such as Check against the standard, Compare two files and Mode of inheritance, write no files. What they found is in the run's record, under the step.
  • A set of files, such as the Phenopackets that One file for each person writes, can only go into a tool that reads several files at once: Put files together, or Put PED files together.

How they differ from Galaxy's

These workflows run in your browser, in the same Python as the tools, and nothing is sent anywhere. Each step is a package's own command, so a run's commands.sh does the same job in a terminal. There are no collections: to run a workflow on several families, run it once for each of them, and each run gets a folder of its own. Galaxy's workflow files can't be opened here, because the tools are different.

A workflow file is JSON. It holds the workflow's name and description, and its steps: each input, and each tool with its settings and the files it reads (Which file is which).

Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets

studio2ga4gh turns a Pedigree Studio drawing into a GA4GH Pedigree and a Phenopackets Family. These are the files you use to share a family's pedigree and findings in the GA4GH standard.

The form

The drawing
Choose a Pedigree Studio file, or the drawing in the Studio tab.
Terms for its conditions
Choose a terms file that gives each condition in the colour key its ontology term (terms files). Under it, a table shows the term each condition will get. A condition that already has an id in the key, such as Condition X [MONDO:0009061], doesn't need a terms file. Only an ontology's id counts, so Hypertension (BP:160/95) is just a name. A condition without a term is left out, because a GA4GH file has no place for a condition without one. Match them to terms…, under the table, opens the tool that finds the missing terms.
People are named by
The default, and the safe choice, is pedigree numbers such as II-3. You can also choose the labels in the drawing, which are often names, or Pedigree Studio's own ids. A label becomes an id in the same way as in studio2ped (J. Mensah becomes J_Mensah, for example), so a person has the same id in both tools' files. Family id
The default, FAM, gives FAM1, then FAM2 and so on if the drawing holds more than one family.
Files to write
You can write both files, or just one of them.
Save in, Start of the file names
The files are called start_ga4gh-pedigree.json and start_phenopackets-family.json. They're saved in ~/results unless you choose another folder.

Drawing → PED or MPED

studio2ped turns a drawing into a PED file, the table of a pedigree that linkage and association programs read. If the drawing shows several conditions or carriers, it writes an MPED file instead, which has a column for each.

The form

The drawing
Choose a Pedigree Studio file, or the drawing in the Studio tab.
Save in, Start of the file name
The file is called start.ped or start.mped. It's saved in ~/results unless you choose another folder.
People are named by
Choose pedigree numbers (II-3), the labels the drawing shows, Pedigree Studio's own ids, or the text under the symbols, which is what version 0.1 used.

What it writes

The file has one line per person. Each line gives the family, the person, their father and mother, and their sex: 1 male, 2 female or 0 unknown. Then, for each condition, there's 1 for unaffected or 2 for affected, and in an MPED file 3 for a carrier.

Each condition's column is named after the condition in the colour key, so Hearing loss from birth becomes Hearing_loss_from_birth. An ontology id written after the name is left out, so Condition X [MONDO:0009061] becomes Condition_X. Only an ontology's id is left out, though. Hypertension (BP:160/95) keeps what's in its brackets, as Hypertension_BP16095. The PED and MPED formats has the details.

The father and mother are the genetic parents, because that's how linkage programs read them. An adopted child, joined to a couple by a dashed line, gets 0 in place of those parents, with a note. So does a child carried by a surrogate (a parent marked S). The Phenopackets Family's pedigree does this too. A donor (D) is a genetic parent, and is written as one.

After writing the file, the tool checks it with mpedtools and shows the result underneath.

The ids

By default, people are named by their pedigree numbers, such as II-3, as in the GA4GH files. The other choices can give you names. The text under a symbol is often a name, and a label may be one too. For example, Grace, 78 becomes Grace_78. Check these ids before you share the file. A label is made into an id by turning spaces into underscores and dropping other punctuation, so J. Mensah becomes J_Mensah. studio2ga4gh does the same, so a person has the same id in both tools' files. If two people would share an id, or an id would be empty or 0 (which a PED file reads as “no parent”), the person's pedigree number is used instead, and the tool tells you.

Pedigree Studio stores the no children and infertility bars as children of the couple. They aren't people, so the tool leaves them out, and the notes say which couple had them. Version 0.1 wrote them as children of unknown sex.

In the terminal

studio2ped /data/examples/cf-cousins.studio.json -d ~/results -o cf-cousins
mpedtools validate ~/results/cf-cousins.mped

Without -o, the file is named after the drawing, leaving out .studio.json, for example cf-cousins.mped. To use one of the other kinds of id, add --ids drawing, --ids studio or --ids text.

    print("wrote", path)

GA4GH Pedigree ⇄ Phenopackets Family

The two GA4GH files describe the same family in two different ways. This tool turns either one into the other. You might use it, for example, when someone has sent you a file in only one of the forms.

The two forms

GA4GH PedigreePhenopackets Family
Who is in iteveryone, each as a Phenopacketthe proband, the relatives with findings, and everyone in its pedigree
How they are relatedrelationships named with KIN terms: parents, donors, adoption, partners, twins, cousins…a PED-style pedigree: father, mother, sex, affected or not
Standardproposed for the GA4GH Pedigree Standard, still being writtenPhenopackets version 2, approved

When you convert to a Family, you lose the narrative and everything a PED-style pedigree can't say: twins, adoptions, separated couples, and the fact that a parent was a donor rather than a partner. When you convert to a GA4GH Pedigree, the relationships come from the parents in the Family's pedigree. The proband's parents are marked as consanguineous if the Family says so and the pedigree doesn't already show it. The GA4GH Pedigree has the details.

The form

The file
Choose a GA4GH Pedigree or a Phenopackets Family.
Make
The tool chooses the other form for you.
Proband
When you're making a Family, give the id of the person you want as the proband. If you don't, the tool uses the GA4GH Pedigree's index patient, or else the first affected person.
Save as
The new file is saved as ~/results/name_phenopackets-family.json or …_ga4gh-pedigree.json.

There are some things that each form can't say:

  • If nothing is recorded for a person in the GA4GH Pedigree, they're in the Family's pedigree, but there's no Phenopacket for them among its relatives. Their status in the Family's pedigree is missing (unknown) rather than unaffected. That's because a GA4GH Pedigree records the conditions people have, not that they were found to have none.
  • Going the other way, people who have no Phenopacket in the Family come into the GA4GH Pedigree with only their sex and parents. The Family's pedigree doesn't say whether they're alive.

So only convert between the two forms when you need to. It's better to keep both of the files that studio2ga4gh writes than to make one from the other.

In the terminal

phenotk convert /data/examples/okafor_ga4gh-pedigree.json --to family -o ~/results/okafor-again_phenopackets-family.json
phenotk convert /data/examples/okafor_phenopackets-family.json --to pedigree -o ~/results/okafor-again_ga4gh-pedigree.json

pedigree-ga4gh convert FILE --to pedigree|family -o OUT does the same.

In Python

GA4GH → PED, table or YAML

This tool writes a GA4GH Pedigree, or a Phenopackets Family, Cohort or Phenopacket, in another format. It can make a PED file for linkage programs, a table of people and their terms, YAML, or JSON Lines.

The formats

A PED file
The six columns are family, person, father, mother, sex and affected status. It uses the file's ids. In files from studio2ga4gh, these are pedigree numbers such as II-3, unless you chose otherwise. If you make it from a Phenopackets Family, the status is the one its pedigree records: 1 for unaffected, 2 for affected. If you make it from a GA4GH Pedigree, people with a condition get 2 and everyone else gets 0, which means unknown. That's because a GA4GH Pedigree records the conditions people have, not that they were found to have none. Either way, affected means having any of the file's conditions. To make a PED file for one condition out of several, use the other route in Make a PED file for a linkage program.
A table (TSV)
The table has a row for each person and term. Each row gives the person's id and their role (proband or relative), and whether the term is a disease or a phenotype. It also gives the term's id and label, and whether it was excluded, which means it was looked for and not found. You can open it in a spreadsheet.
YAML
This is the same document, written in YAML instead of JSON.
JSON Lines
This puts one Phenopacket on each line, for programs that read many at a time.

In the terminal

phenotk convert /data/examples/okafor_phenopackets-family.json --to ped -o ~/results/okafor.ped
phenotk convert /data/examples/okafor_ga4gh-pedigree.json --to tsv -o ~/results/okafor.tsv
column -t -s "$(printf '\t')" ~/results/okafor.tsv

--to takes ped, tsv, yaml, jsonl and json, as well as family or pedigree for the other GA4GH form. phenotk ped FILE also writes the PED file.

Picture → PED file and drawing

PedInterp reads a picture of a pedigree, such as one in a paper, a clinic letter or a scan, or one sketched by hand, and writes it as a PED file and a Pedigree Studio drawing, and as a GA4GH Pedigree if you ask. It finds the symbols and the lines between them, and so who is male or female, who is affected, who are partners, parents and children, and which children are twins, and, if you ask, who has died, the proband and carriers. So a pedigree sketched in a hurry, with the Studio's Draw tool or on paper, becomes a drawing you can carry on with in the Studio.

The form

The picture
A PNG or JPEG file, or a GIF, BMP, TIFF or WebP picture. From my computer… adds one of your own, in ~/pictures.
Drawn by hand
Tick it for a pedigree sketched by hand, in whole or in part: with Pedigree Studio's Draw tool, say, or begun with the Studio's symbols and finished with the Draw tool, or drawn on paper and scanned. PedInterp then allows for symbols of unequal sizes, outlines that don't quite close or that run on past where they began, symbols filled in by scribbling, and lines that wobble and stop short of what they join or run a little past it. Leave it unticked for a picture drawn with software, such as one Pedigree Studio saves, which reads more surely so. If PedInterp warns that lines may have been missed in a picture read without it, and says the pedigree may be drawn by hand, try again with it ticked.
Read who has died, the proband and carriers
Tick it to read three of the marks drawn on the symbols as well as their shapes and fills: a slash through the symbol of someone who has died, an arrow pointing at the proband's symbol from below on the left, and a dot in the middle of a carrier's symbol. A PED file can't hold them, so with it ticked no PED file is written: tick Make a Pedigree Studio drawing too or Write a GA4GH Pedigree too, or both, to keep them. The table shown has a column for each, True or False. See Who has died, the proband and carriers, below.
Family id
The family's id in the PED file, which also names the files. It starts as the picture's name.
Save in
The folder for the files: ~/results, unless you choose another.
Enlarge the picture by
Leave it empty, as it is, and PedInterp chooses: it finds the symbols in a copy of the picture 2,000 pixels across, and enlarges the picture so that they are about 200 pixels across, which reads best (100 for a sketch, about the size the Studio draws them at). A line under the table says how much it enlarged it: 5.6 times for the Lynch family's picture, which is about 1,000 pixels across, and more for a smaller picture, less for a larger one or a scan. There is some room either side: the Lynch picture reads correctly at anything from 4 to 12 times, but not at 3 or 15. If PedInterp warns that a line may have been missed, give a number a little larger or smaller than the one it chose: smaller, if the lines are very thick. It warns, too, if the picture is too small to enlarge enough, or a page too large, which reads better cut down to the pedigree.
Make a Pedigree Studio drawing too
It starts ticked. The family as a Pedigree Studio drawing, FAMILY.studio.json, beside the PED file (or, with the marks read, without one), to open in the Studio tab (Open the drawing in Studio, under the result) and carry on with: put right anything misread, add names, deaths and adoptions, and save it. It is laid out as the picture is: each row of symbols is a generation, at the Studio's spacing, and everyone keeps their place, so the drawing looks like the picture, tidied. A sketch drawn small or crowded is spread out, so that the Studio's numbers have room, and the Studio's Tidy lays it out afresh if you'd rather. The drawing has what a PED file can't hold: twins, identical or not, joined as the Studio joins them, and couples who have no children. A pregnancy that was lost is the Studio's triangle, and couples who are related, such as cousins who marry, get the Studio's double line. The affected are filled in, and the colour key says Affected, or names the condition if you give one. With Read who has died, the proband and carriers, the drawing has the slashes, the proband's arrow and the carriers' dots too.
Write a GA4GH Pedigree too
The family as a GA4GH Pedigree, FAMILY_ga4gh-pedigree.json, beside the PED file (or without one), which ga4gh2studio, the phenopacket toolkit, PedNet and the Risk tab read. The affected people have the condition, and the unaffected have it marked as looked for and not found. It records the twins too, and with Read who has died, the proband and carriers, who has died and who is alive, the proband, as its index patient, and in its narrative, who is a carrier (see below). The file notes which picture the family was read from, and how much it was enlarged.
The condition's ontology term, and its name
Shown when the drawing or the GA4GH Pedigree is ticked: the condition the pedigree is about, such as MONDO:0007254 and breast cancer. The GA4GH Pedigree gives it to the affected, and the drawing's colour key names it with its term, breast cancer [MONDO:0007254], as ga4gh2studio names it, so that the drawing converts back to the same term (Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets). Leave them empty for MONDO:0000001, disease, in the GA4GH Pedigree, and Affected in the colour key.

What it reads

PedInterp reads pedigrees drawn in the usual way, dark on white: squares, circles and diamonds, filled in for the people who are affected, with each generation in a row of its own. It follows the lines between partners, down from partners to their children, and over a family of brothers and sisters. A slash through a symbol, for someone who has died, doesn't usually stop it reading the symbol, though a slash at an odd angle can make it mistake the person's sex; and only if you ask does it read the slash itself (see Who has died, the proband and carriers, below). The father is the male partner and the mother the female one, whichever side each is drawn on. Pedigree Studio's numbers over the symbols, in grey, are taken out before the lines are read, even where they touch a line.

Twins are two children whose lines come down from one point, and identical twins have a bar across between the two lines. A PED file has no place for twins, so the PED file leaves them out, and so does the table shown when the tool has run, which is the PED file's (with a column for each mark, when they are read). The drawing and the GA4GH Pedigree record them, as Pedigree Studio's files do. The Okafor family has identical twins, IV-3 and IV-4.

With Drawn by hand, PedInterp reads pedigrees sketched roughly, as testers in a clinic sketch them with the Studio's Draw tool: in any colour, with symbols of unequal sizes, filled in with scribbles, and lines that wobble. Of 48 sketches made with the Draw tool by a program that imitates a hand, for testing, it read 40 exactly right, 445 of their 459 people right, and 20 of their 23 pairs of twins; of 24 pictures drawn with the Studio's symbols, it read every person and every pair of twins right. Twins are what it most often gets wrong in a sketch, as the bar of identical twins is short and drawn close to the other lines, and a line drawn close beside a symbol filled in with scribbles can be taken for part of it. The Okafor sketch here is one it reads right.

Some things aren't read yet: symbols that are half filled or shaded (as they sometimes are for carriers), and dashed lines. A PED file has no place for deaths, twins or adoptions anyway. So compare what PedInterp read with the picture before you use it, and a sketch most of all. The drawing is the quickest way: under the result, Open the drawing in Studio shows it laid out as the picture is, where anything misread is easy to see and to put right. If you put something right, make the PED file again from the drawing with Drawing → PED or MPED. Without the drawing, Draw it makes one from the GA4GH Pedigree, if you wrote one (GA4GH or Phenopackets → drawing), or from the PED file (PED or MPED → drawing), laid out afresh.

Who has died, the proband and carriers

With Read who has died, the proband and carriers ticked, PedInterp reads three marks on the symbols:

  • A slash through the symbol, rising to the right, for someone who has died. In a symbol left empty, it is seen inside the symbol. A symbol filled in hides the slash, so there it is read from where it runs out past the symbol at both ends, as a slash drawn by hand usually does, and as the Studio draws one through a circle or a diamond. The Studio draws the slash through a filled square from corner to corner, inside it, where it can't be seen, so that one isn't read.
  • An arrow pointing at the proband's symbol from below on the left, with its head by the symbol, as the Studio draws it. Drawn by hand, it reads best drawn clear of the lines.
  • A dot in the middle of a carrier's symbol, thicker than the lines: dark in a symbol left empty, and white in one filled in, as the Studio draws it. A dot can't be seen in a symbol scribbled in.

Other marks aren't read: the upright line of a presymptomatic carrier, the letters the Studio writes in a symbol (P, D and S), which aren't taken for a dot, the brackets round someone adopted, and what is written beside a symbol, such as an age or a cause of death. The Okafor family's picture, as the Studio saves it, reads with its five deaths and its proband (Chidi, IV-3), among the names and ages written under the symbols, and so does the sketch of it, with Drawn by hand.

The marks were tested on 192 pedigrees drawn with the Studio and its Draw tool, a quarter with its symbols alone, a quarter begun with them and finished by hand, and half sketched throughout. Of the Studio's own marks, PedInterp found every death that can be seen (121; the other 19 were slashes through filled squares), every proband (54) and every carrier (92). Of the marks drawn by hand, it found 226 of 253 deaths, 127 of 130 carriers, and 66 of 102 probands: in the sketches, 44 of 53 arrows drawn clear of the other lines, but only 8 of 30 that crossed or touched another line or the end of a slash. It read no mark where none was drawn, and the rest of each pedigree as well as it reads the same pedigree drawn without the marks. (These counts leave out the people in a generation read with a person too many or too few, whose numbers then don't match those drawn: 62 of the 1,969.) Nor does it take a symbol shaded with the Studio's dots, or specks in a poor scan, for one with a slash.

A PED file can't hold the marks, so with them ticked, the tool writes no PED file: only the drawing, the GA4GH Pedigree or both, as you tick them. The drawing has the marks as the Studio draws them, and the GA4GH Pedigree as Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets writes them: the proband as the index patient, who is a carrier in its narrative, and each person's vital status. Someone has died if a slash was read through their symbol, or if it is a triangle, for a pregnancy lost, and is alive if their symbol is left empty with no slash through it. A symbol filled in may hide a slash, as it hides the Studio's through a filled square, so the GA4GH Pedigree gives no vital status for the affected unless a slash was seen.

Who is who

On a computer of your own, PedInterp reads the labels under the symbols with the Tesseract program. A browser can't run Tesseract, so here the people are numbered by generation instead: from the top, and from the left in each generation, I-1, I-2, II-1 and so on. Pedigree Studio numbers people in the same way, and shows the numbers over the symbols in the drawing. The picture of the Lynch family was drawn where the Studio draws the family, so its numbers are the same as those in the Lynch PED file, and the PED file that PedInterp writes is the same as that one, apart from the family's id. The two pictures of the Okafor family read as the people, parents and sexes of okafor.mped, with those who have either of its conditions affected, as a picture shows who is affected but not by what.

In Python

from PedInterp import pedigree_processing, PED_export, Studio_export

df = pedigree_processing("LYNCH", image_path="/data/examples/lynch.png", ocr=None)
print(df.to_string(index=False))
print("wrote", PED_export(df, "/home/user/results"))

sketch = pedigree_processing("OKAFOR", image_path="/data/examples/okafor-sketch.png", ocr=None, hand_drawn=True)
print("wrote", Studio_export(sketch, "/home/user/results"))   # OKAFOR.studio.json, with its identical twins
print(sketch.attrs["twins"])   # [["IV-3", "IV-4", "MZ"]]: MZ for identical twins, DZ for twins who aren't

marked = pedigree_processing("OKAFOR", image_path="/data/examples/okafor-sketch.png", ocr=None, hand_drawn=True,
                             marks=True)
print(marked[["IndividualID", "Deceased", "Proband", "Carrier"]].to_string(index=False))

ocr=None numbers the people, hand_drawn=True reads a sketch, and marks=True reads who has died, the proband and carriers, in the columns Deceased, Proband and Carrier: PED_export refuses such a table, and Studio_export and GA4GH_export write it. Besides the twins, attrs holds the couples PedInterp found (attrs["partners"]) and where each person was drawn (attrs["layout"]), which Studio_export lays the drawing out by. Studio_export takes a condition as condition=("MONDO:0007254", "breast cancer"), as GA4GH_export does. ocr can also be a function that reads the labels with other OCR, such as tesseract.js in a web page: PedInterp's README explains how.

Match conditions to terms

This tool finds the ontology term for each condition in a drawing's colour key: a Mondo term for a disease, or an HPO term for a sign or symptom. It keeps your choices in a terms file, which Drawing → GA4GH reads. It uses the same searches as hponorm and mondonorm.

On the page

1 · The drawing
The drawing's conditions are listed under 3, with the number of people who have each one. Start from a terms file fills in the conditions that the terms file already has, and the matches you make are added to it when you save.
2 · Search in
Choose EBI's Ontology Lookup Service, which is online and has the latest releases, or copies of the ontologies on this computer, which work offline. For the copies, choose hp.obo and mondo.obo from your files. You can download the HPO from here into ~/ontologies. For Mondo, download its file with your browser from Mondo's download page, then add it with From my computer….
3 · The conditions
Each condition has a menu to choose a disease (Mondo) or a sign or symptom (HPO), a box for the words to search for, and a Suggest button. The tool guesses which kind it is from the name, so check it. You get up to six suggestions, each with its id and label, a score out of 100 for how well it matches, and the definition. Each also says what your words matched: the term's label, or one of its synonyms. Choose one, or type an id and press Use this id. Suggest terms for all searches for every condition that hasn't been matched yet.
4 · Keep the matches
Save writes the terms file, ~/terms/drawing-terms.json. If you tick Also write the ids into the drawing's colour key, it also writes a copy of the drawing, ~/pedigrees/drawing-with-terms.studio.json. In the copy, the key has entries such as Breast cancer [MONDO:0007254]. Next opens Drawing → GA4GH with the drawing and the terms file already chosen.

What is sent where

If you use EBI's service, the words you search for and the ids you type are sent to EBI (www.ebi.ac.uk). Nothing else is sent, not even the drawing. If you use copies on this computer, nothing is sent anywhere. Downloading the HPO here fetches hp.obo from the HPO's repository on GitHub.

Good to know

  • The first search of a local file reads the whole file, which takes a few seconds for the HPO and longer for Mondo. The local search can miss terms that EBI's service would find, so if the term you expect is missing, try other words.
  • Each term's release is recorded with it in the terms file, and goes into the GA4GH files' metaData. That way, anyone can tell which version of the ontology the term came from.
  • If you type an id, the tool looks it up if it can. If it can't, because there's no copy of that ontology here or EBI doesn't answer, the id is used as you typed it. It's labelled with the condition's name, and the release is left out.
  • If the key itself already has an id, as in Condition X [MONDO:0009061], that condition counts as matched.

Give the conditions ontology terms walks you through it, with advice on choosing a term.

In Python

There's no command for this page. hponorm and mondonorm work on GA4GH and Phenopackets files, and ask about each name as they go. You can't answer their questions in the terminal, so there they take their answers from a file. You can use their searches in a notebook:

Fill in missing terms

This tool gives an ontology id to every disease and phenotype in a GA4GH or Phenopackets file that has a name but no id. It takes the id from a terms file, or looks the name up. It also writes the file in the standard form, with the ontology resources that its terms need.

The form

The file
Choose a GA4GH Pedigree, or a Phenopackets Family, Cohort or Phenopacket. A file from an older tool must be upgraded first.
Terms file
Choose a file of names and their terms (terms files). Capital letters don't matter when the names are matched. The releases in the terms file are recorded in the new file's resources.
Look up names without a term
Choose No to use only the terms file, or look names up on EBI's Ontology Lookup Service, which is online, or in copies of the ontologies on this computer. For the copies, choose hp.obo and mondo.obo.
Accept a looked-up match from
This is the lowest score, from 0.5 to 1, at which a term the tool finds is used. A term's own name scores 1 and one of its synonyms 0.98, ignoring capital letters and punctuation, and any other name scores less. So the default, 0.98, only accepts a term whose own name or synonym is the condition's name. Match conditions to terms shows the same scores out of 100. If a name's best match is less close than that, it's left without a term and listed. Entries in a terms file are always used.
Save as
The new file is saved as ~/results/name.normalised.json. A log of every decision goes next to it, as ….normalised.log.json.

By default, the tool doesn't guess. If neither the terms file nor a look-up finds a term for a name, its id stays empty and the summary lists it. You can lower the threshold to accept close matches as well, but each of those is a guess, and the log asks you to check it. Names that are spelt almost alike can mean opposite things. For example, Maternal hypothyroidism is not Maternal hyperthyroidism. Look at the log before you rely on a looked-up match.

Each term goes where its kind belongs. Older tools wrote every condition as a disease, so a “disease” that's given an HPO term, such as Hearing loss from birth (HP:0000365), moves to the phenotypic features. In the same way, a phenotype that's given a Mondo term moves to the diseases. The summary notes each move.

Check against the standard

This tool checks a GA4GH Pedigree, or a Phenopackets Family, Cohort or Phenopacket. For each problem it finds, it tells you what the problem is, where it is in the file and how to fix it. It works from the file alone, without needing Java or an internet connection.

What is checked

The phenopacket-tools rules

phenopacket-tools is the validator for Phenopackets written by the team behind the Phenopackets schema. This check applies the same rules:

  • The Phenopackets version 2 schema: which fields each part must have and which it may have, their types, the values allowed (for sex, vital status, progress status …), groups of fields where exactly one must be given, and minimum lengths and counts. A field that isn't in the schema is an error, because programs that read Phenopackets reject it.
  • The ontologies: every term's id must be a CURIE (PREFIX:ID), and every prefix used must be declared among the resources in the file's metaData, with all six of the resource's fields filled in. phenopacket-tools treats an empty field as a missing one.
  • Values: ages must be ISO 8601 durations (P45Y), and dates and times must be in the right form.

And for a GA4GH Pedigree

  • Every id in the relationships and the index patients must refer to the subject of one of the individuals, and no two individuals may share an id.
  • Every relation must be a current KIN term, written with KIN's own label. Retired codes from earlier drafts of KIN are reported.
  • No one may be related to themselves. A biological mother must be female, and a father male. No one may have more than two genetic parents, or be their own ancestor.

And for a Family

  • Everyone with a Phenopacket, whether the proband or a relative, must be in the pedigree. No one may be in it twice or have two Phenopackets.
  • Every father and mother named in the pedigree must also be a person in it. A father mustn't be recorded as female, or a mother as male. No one may be both someone's father and their mother, or be their own ancestor.
  • No one's id may be 0, which a PED file uses for “no parent”. If someone had that id, their children would read as having no parent.

And one mistake in older files

  • HP:0032500 used for a carrier. It's a real HPO term, so it's valid in form, but it means Exacerbated by tobacco use.

The check doesn't look terms up, so it can't tell whether a term is the right one for the person. It can only tell you that the term is well formed and declared.

The result

You'll see Valid or Not valid, then a line for each finding. An error means the file is invalid, a warning means something is allowed but probably a mistake, and a note is for information. Each finding has a code, such as empty-term-id, its place in the file, and how to fix it. If a file comes from an older tool, the check recognises this and gives you a button to upgrade it. Check a GA4GH file, or update an old one lists the commonest findings and their fixes.

In the terminal

pedigree-ga4gh validate --json /data/examples/older/abusua2ga4gh-0.2_phenopackets-family.json | head -20

The exit status is 0 for a valid file and 1 for an invalid one. --json prints the findings as JSON. phenotk validate FILE runs the same checks on a file in the standard form. It refuses a file from an older tool, with a message telling you to upgrade it.

Upgrade a file from an older tool

Earlier versions of our tools, studio2ga4gh up to 0.3 and abusua2ga4gh up to 0.2, wrote GA4GH files that the standard doesn't accept. The upgrade rewrites a file like this once, in the standard form, and lists every change it made and everything it couldn't do. It tidies up other non-standard files too.

What it changes

  • The form. The GA4GH Pedigree from studio2ga4gh 0.3 was the conceptual model written out as JSON. The upgrade turns it into one Phenopacket per person, with the relationships in the standard form.
  • What the standard has no place for. Names are removed, because Phenopackets have no field for a name, and names are personal data. Diseases inside subject are moved to the Phenopacket, and notes on relationships go into the narrative.
  • metaData. The upgrade adds it where it's missing, with phenopacketSchemaVersion and a resource for every ontology the terms use.
  • KIN. Retired codes are replaced by the current ones, and the two people are swapped where the new term runs the other way. Labels are corrected. If a relation's code and label disagree, the label decides. For example, abusua2ga4gh's KIN:039 isSpermDonorOf becomes KIN:004.
  • Carriers. Where the phenotypic feature HP:0032500 was written for a carrier, it's removed, because it means Exacerbated by tobacco use. In a GA4GH Pedigree, the narrative then says who is a carrier.
  • Field names in proto spelling are rewritten in camelCase, the way JSON spells them, so index_patients becomes indexPatients.

What it leaves to you

Under to do, the upgrade lists the things that only a person can decide:

  • Conditions that have a name but no term. Fill in missing terms gives them their terms.
  • Carriers in a Phenopacket or a Family. Phenopackets have no field for carrier status, and the only structured place for it is a genomic interpretation of the variant, if the variant is known. You can add one with phenotk add --variant in the terminal (Add findings to people).
  • Foster and social parents that abusua2ga4gh wrote as adoptive parents. Only the relationship's note tells them apart.
  • The conditions in a GA4GH Pedigree from studio2ga4gh 0.3. That file records who was affected, but not by what, so convert the drawing again, or use the Phenopackets file that was made with it.

The upgrade never guesses. A file that's already standard comes back unchanged, apart from its field names. Under the result, Check the upgraded file runs the check on the new file.

A file that follows the draft of version 1 of the standard isn't an older file. It's in a newer form, which hasn't been approved (see The GA4GH Pedigree). The upgrade tells you this and leaves the file alone.

Compare two files

This tool lists what differs between two GA4GH or Phenopackets files, for example in their people, terms and relationships. It ignores the order of things. It also ignores when and by whom the files were made, because that changes every time a file is written.

When to use it

  • To see what a conversion changed, by comparing a file with the same file converted to the other form and back.
  • To see what's new in a second version of a family's file.
  • To check that a de-identified or filtered file lost only what it should have.

Reading the result

You'll see a line for each difference, with its place in the files, such as proband.phenotypicFeatures: length 1 != 2, pedigree.persons[3].affectedStatus: 'UNAFFECTED' != 'MISSING' or narrative: only in A. De-identify

This tool makes a copy of a GA4GH or Phenopackets file for sharing. Everyone gets a new id, which is used consistently everywhere in the file. Dates of birth and other ids are taken out, and you can also hide who made the file. You can keep the key from the old ids to the new ones in a separate file.

The form

New ids
Choose IND-0001, IND-0002 … to number the people in the order they come in the file. With codes made from the old ids (such as IND-3f9a1c2b7d40), a person gets the same code in every file, as long as you use the same secret. This means you can follow the same person across files without their id. Or you can keep the ids as they are.
Secret for the codes
Use any words you like, and keep them private. Without a secret, anyone who has the old ids could make the codes again, and so match each code to its old id.
Keep dates of birth, Ages
Dates of birth are taken out unless you choose to keep them. You can put the age at the last encounter into bands of 5 or 10 years. For example, in 5-year bands, 47 becomes 45. Ages of onset aren't changed.
Keep other ids
A person's other ids (alternateIds, such as a hospital number) are taken out unless you choose to keep them.
Hide who made the file and when
The metaData has to say who made the file and when. So instead of the original createdBy and created, it says phenotk deidentify and the time of the de-identification. The record of updates and submittedBy are taken out.
Save the key as
This is a JSON file of old id → new id. Keep it apart from the shared file, because with it, anyone can follow the new ids back to the old ones.

What changes in the file

  • The ids change everywhere: in each Phenopacket and its subject, the relationships, the index patient, a Family's pedigree, biosamples, genomic interpretations and files.
  • The ids in a GA4GH Pedigree's narrative change too. For example, proband IV-3 becomes proband IND-0014. The narrative is free text, so read it before you share the file. The tool reminds you to do this. If some of the ids are bare numbers, the tool can't tell them from other numbers in the text, so it leaves the narrative unchanged and warns you.
  • Nothing else changes. Terms, sexes, relationships and the shape of the family stay as they were.
De-identified is not anonymous

De-identifying removes what the file says directly. But a family can still be recognised from what's left, such as a rare condition, a large or unusual family, people's ages or where it came from. Someone who knows the family is especially likely to recognise it. It's for you to judge whether a file can be shared, and with whom, under your institution's rules and the law on patient data.

This tool merges Phenopackets, Families and GA4GH Pedigrees into one Cohort or into one Family. A Cohort is a set of people who don't have to be related.

Into

Its id, Save as

PED and MPED files

mpedtools works with PED files, and with MPED files, which are PED files with a column for each of several conditions. Its five tools in the Tools tab summarise and check a file, take one condition out, put files together, split families apart, and turn one kind of file into the other.

The five tools

Summary and check
This lists the families and the people (founders and the rest, by sex), and for each condition, how many people are affected, unaffected and carriers. Then it checks for people listed twice, parents who aren't in the file, parents of the wrong sex, and status codes other than 0, 1, 2 and 3.
Take one condition out
This makes a plain PED file for one condition of an MPED file. Give the condition's column name, as the file's first line or the summary shows it. If you leave the name empty, you get a file for each condition. Carriers (3) become unaffected (1), because a plain PED file has no code for them.
Put PED files together
This puts PED files of the same people, with one condition each, into one MPED file. You can name the conditions, in the same order as the files. If you don't, each one is named after its file.
One file for each family
This splits a PED or MPED file of several families into a file for each family, named after the family's id.
PED ⇄ MPED, and columns
This turns a PED file into an MPED file, or the other way round, keeping one condition. It can also add or remove a condition column. A new column starts with everyone at the value you choose.

PedNet: the graph, the mode of inheritance and the variants

PedNet analyses a pedigree as a graph: a directed acyclic graph, with a node for each person and an edge from each parent to each child. Its three tools in the Tools tab show how a family is put together, weigh up how likely each mode of inheritance is, and rank candidate variants by how well each co-segregates with the condition.

All three read a PED file, a GA4GH Pedigree or a Phenopackets Family. From an MPED file, they take the first condition, and a carrier (3) counts as unknown. A drawing has to be made into one of these first, with Drawing → PED or MPED or Drawing → GA4GH Pedigree and Phenopackets.

GA4GH files

PedNet reads a GA4GH Pedigree or a Phenopackets Family with pedigree-ga4gh, as the other tools do, so it finds the same parents: a sperm or egg donor counts as a parent, and an adoptive parent doesn't. A GA4GH Pedigree says who is affected through each person's conditions. Two more fields appear in the form when you choose a GA4GH file:

Condition
For a file that names several conditions, the one that makes someone affected, by its ontology id or its name, such as HP:0000365 or Hearing impairment. Without one, anyone with any of the file's conditions is affected, and from a Phenopackets Family, the Family's own record of who is affected is used. The result says which.
People with no record of the condition
A GA4GH Pedigree records who has a condition, but only sometimes who doesn't. Unless you choose, PedNet takes the people with no record of it as unaffected if no one in the file is recorded as not having it, as in the files studio2ga4gh writes from drawings, and as not known if anyone is, as in the files PedGen and PedInterp write, which record both.

The pedigree as a graph

For each family in the file, this lists:

  • its loops, each with the people round it. A loop through a couple who are related is from consanguinity, and a loop through no related couple is from marriage: two families who married into each other more than once.
  • who is inbred, with their inbreeding coefficient, F: the chance that the two copies of a gene they have are copies of one copy in an ancestor of both their parents. The children of first cousins have F = 1/16 = 0.0625.
  • how broad and how tangled it is: its width, the largest number of people none of whom is an ancestor of another, and its elimination width, which sets how much work the exact likelihoods of the other two tools take. Only a pedigree with very many loops is too tangled for them.
  • the closest pairs of affected relatives, each with the name of their relationship (brother, first cousin once removed), its degree, and their kinship coefficient: the chance that a copy of a gene taken at random from one of them and a copy taken from the other are the same copy, inherited from an ancestor they share.
  • network measures: the original PedNet's measures of where the affected people are, the features of their pattern that the classifier of the mode reads, and the people most central to the graph.

It can also save everyone's kinship coefficients as a table, for the autosomes or for the X chromosome, and the graph as GraphML, which Gephi, Cytoscape and other network tools open. The mating graph has a node for each couple as well, between the partners and their children. Both need one family, so if the file holds several, give the family.

Mode of inheritance

This weighs up autosomal dominant (AD), autosomal recessive (AR), X-linked dominant (XLD) and X-linked recessive (XLR) inheritance, and gives the probability of each, with the probabilities that the condition is dominant or recessive, and autosomal or X-linked. A mode is named only when its probability reaches the threshold, 0.8 unless you change it. Otherwise the answer is uncertain. The third method, network measures, works differently.

Exact likelihoods (the default)
PedNet treats the pedigree as a Bayesian network of everyone's genotypes, and works out exactly how likely it is that just these people are affected under each mode, averaged over four penetrances, 0.6, 0.8, 0.95 and 0.99, unless you give others, with a chance of 0.001 of being affected without the disease genotype. A family comes to notice through someone affected, the proband, so the likelihoods are worked out given that the proband has the disease genotype. If you don't say who the proband is, each affected person is taken in turn. A pattern that only a new mutation would explain comes out unlikely, because PedNet doesn't model new mutations. With several families that have the same condition, tick The families have the same condition to get one answer from them all.
The pattern of affected people
A classifier that learnt, from thousands of simulated families, where the affected people sit in the graph under each mode: in every generation or skipping some, passed from father to son, mostly male, the children of related parents, and so on.
Network measures
The original PedNet's classifier, which tells dominant from recessive by a vote. Two of the four network measures it shows have a vote each: the share of affected people who have an affected parent, and the share of generations in which someone is affected. A third vote goes to dominant inheritance, unless an affected person has two unaffected parents, which gives two votes to recessive inheritance. The classifier calls a condition dominant when all three votes say so, and recessive when it has three votes or more for recessive inheritance. Otherwise, it says uncertain. It gives no probabilities, so the threshold doesn't apply to it.

The likelihoods are the method to rely on. The classifiers show what the pattern alone suggests. PedNet takes a condition to be rare: it gives the allele a frequency of 1 in 1,000 for autosomal recessive inheritance, 1 in 2,000 for X-linked recessive, and 1 in 10,000 for the dominant modes. In Python, infer_mode takes other frequencies, as in allele_frequency={"AR": 0.01}.

Rank candidate variants

For each candidate variant, PedNet works out how likely everyone's genotypes and the pattern of affected people are if the variant causes the condition, and if it doesn't. The ratio of the two is a Bayes factor. Its strength as evidence for co-segregation (PP1) or against it (BS4), under the ACMG/AMP guidelines, is given as Supporting, Moderate or Strong: for co-segregation at Bayes factors of 2.08, 4.33 and 18.7, the odds of the guidelines as a Bayesian framework, and against it at 1/2.08, 1/4.33 and 1/18.7. On the whole, a variant scores higher the more of the affected people carry it and the fewer of the unaffected do, but who the carriers are matters too, and genotypes that can't have come from the parents count heavily against a variant.

The variants are ranked by their odds of being the cause: the Bayes factor times the probability, from who is affected, that the condition is inherited in a way that suits the variant's chromosome. So a variant on the X chromosome ranks low in a family whose condition is clearly autosomal. The table gives both, as log10 odds and log10 BF. Pairs of variants in one gene that an affected person carries both of are scored too, as compound heterozygotes under the recessive modes. Genotypes that break Mendel's laws are counted, and PedNet names anyone who carries a variant that their parents were found not to carry (on the X chromosome, a son whose mother was found not to carry it), as a new mutation in them would explain that genotype, though not necessarily the others.

The variants
A VCF file, plain or gzipped, whose samples are named with the PED file's ids; or a table with a row for each variant and a column for each person, holding the number of copies of the variant (0, 1 or 2) or a call such as 0/1. A table names its variants in a column variant, and can also have the columns chrom, pos, ref, alt, af (the allele frequency) and gene. Simulate a family writes one.
Modes of inheritance
All four, weighed up as in Mode of inheritance; the two autosomal ones; or the one you know.
Leave out variants commoner than
An allele frequency. Variants with no frequency in the file are kept.
How many to show, Save every variant's scores
The table under the result shows the best 20, unless you change it. The table you save, separated by tabs, has every variant, with every score.

If the PED file holds several families, they're taken to have the same condition, and each variant is ranked by its evidence across the families in which an affected person carries it.

Simulate a family

PedGen makes up a family with an autosomal dominant or recessive condition, by Mendelian inheritance from an affected founder, and writes it as a PED file, and as a GA4GH Pedigree if you ask. Use it for teaching, or to try PedNet or the Risk tab on families whose mode of inheritance you know.

How it works

The founder has the condition. In each generation, every child gets one of each parent's two copies of the gene, at random, and each couple has between one child and the most you allow. In every generation but the last, the first child of a couple always has a partner and children, and their brothers and sisters each have them with the chance you give, so that the family has as many generations as you ask for. Partners come from a population in which the condition's allele has the frequency you give, a rare one unless you change it, and some come with their own parents, and theirs. Who is affected follows from the genotypes: one copy of the allele is enough for a dominant condition, and a recessive one needs two. As everyone's genotype is known, PedGen can write them too, as a candidate variant for Rank candidate variants.

A rare recessive condition

With a rare allele, someone who marries into the family almost never carries it, so a recessive condition seldom turns up again in the family unless relatives marry. That is so of rare recessive diseases too, which are commoner in the children of cousins. So for a recessive condition, the form lets cousins marry whenever they can, and makes families until one has at least two people affected, unless you change these. Cousins are first found among the founder's grandchildren, in the third generation, so a recessive family needs four generations or more. At the default frequency, only about one four-generation family in fifty has a second person affected, so PedGen makes about fifty families, on average, before it keeps one, which takes a few seconds.

The Risk tab

The Risk tab works out the chance that someone in a family carries the family's variant, or will be affected, and the chance that a couple's child will be affected. Like a Bayesian risk calculation by hand, it takes account of everything known of the family, but it does so for the whole family at once, exactly, under the model of inheritance you choose. Below the answers, By hand has the four squares of a cross and a calculator that multiplies chances together, for working out the simpler cases yourself.

Work out the risk to a pregnancy takes you through three of the example families, step by step.

The family

How it is inherited

  • Mode of inheritance: autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive or X-linked dominant. The chances depend on it, so if you aren't sure of the mode, compare the answers under each. PedNet's Mode of inheritance weighs up which mode the pattern of affected people suggests.
  • How common the variant is: the allele frequency, which is the share of the copies of the gene in the population that have the variant, or else how many people carry it. It matters most for the people who married into the family. When 1 in 25 people carry the variant, as for cystic fibrosis in northern Europe, an unaffected partner from the population is a carrier with a chance of 1 in 25. For a rare variant, it hardly changes the chances of the people born into the family.
  • Penetrance: the share of the people with the variant (with two copies, under a recessive mode) who are affected. Below 100%, someone unaffected may still have the variant.
  • Phenocopies and new mutations, which open under their own heading: the share of the people without the variant who are affected for another reason, and how often a new copy of the variant arises in an egg or a sperm. Both are 0 unless you give them.

Sometimes what is known of a family can't happen under the model you've chosen. For example, under a dominant mode with full penetrance and no new mutations, two unaffected parents can't have an affected child. The tab then says who can't be as they are, and what you could change.

The answers

One person
The person's chance of being a carrier, or of having the variant under a dominant mode or for a man under an X-linked one, and, if their own status isn't known, of being or becoming affected. Under these chances is the Bayesian table they come from. For each genotype, the table gives the prior chance, from the rest of the family; the conditional chance of what is known of the person and their descendants; the joint chance, which is the two multiplied together; and the posterior chance, which is the joint divided by the sum of the joints. How the chances are worked out explains it in full.
A child of a couple
The chance that a child of a couple who isn't born yet will be affected, and will be a carrier, given for a son and for a daughter under an X-linked mode. You can choose the parents of any child in the family, or a couple drawn as partners who have no children yet. If you choose someone in the family, and a partner from the population, the tab gives that person an unaffected partner who isn't related to them.
Everyone
Each person's chances, in a table. Click a name to see that person's chances and their Bayesian table above. With Test result or status, you can give someone a test result, such as a carrier or doesn't have it, and see how it changes everyone's chances. The Risk tab takes a test result to be certain. You can also give someone a status here: affected or unaffected, for someone the file has no record of, or not known, which leaves out whether they are affected, for someone too young to show a condition that starts later in life. These are used only in the Risk tab. They're kept in this browser for your next visit, but they aren't added to the family's file. By hand

By hand, at the bottom of the tab, shows what each child of a couple can inherit, drawn as chromosomes in a square of four equally likely children, for autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant and X-linked recessive inheritance. Under it, a calculator multiplies chances together into the chance that a baby is affected. For example, the child of the unaffected brother of someone with cystic fibrosis and a partner from the population has a chance of 2/3 × 1/25 × 1/4 = 1/150. Multiplying is right when each chance comes from a different part of the family. The rest of the tab works out every chance from the whole family at once, so it allows for what each person's relatives tell you about them as well.

What the chances leave out

The chances are exact for the model, but only as good as the model and what you know of the family. The model has one gene, with the same penetrance at every age and for men and women. It doesn't allow for a condition that more than one gene can cause, for linked markers, for mosaicism in a parent, or for a parent who isn't the genetic parent shown in the pedigree. Nor does it allow for age, although you can take the status of someone too young to show the condition as not known. For a condition that men who have it don't pass on, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy, make sure that the men who had children are unaffected, giving them that status under Everyone if the file has no record of it: a man whose status isn't known might have the variant, and that changes the chances of the women descended from him. A test that can miss a variant should be left out, or allowed for with a lower penetrance, because the tab takes every test result to be right. For inherited breast, ovarian and other cancers, use a model made for them, such as CanRisk, which allows for age and for several genes. Use the Risk tab to learn and to check your own sums, and for a real family, work with a clinical genetics service.

How the chances are worked out

The Risk tab works out each chance exactly, with Bayes' theorem, from a model of how the variant is passed on and of how it shows itself. This page describes the model and the sums, explains the Bayesian table, and says how the sums were checked.

The model

  • Genotypes. Each person has 0, 1 or 2 copies of the variant. Under an X-linked mode, a man has one X chromosome, and so has 0 copies or 1. Someone whose sex isn't known, such as an early pregnancy, may be a boy or a girl, with a chance of 1/2 each. Identical twins have the same genotype.
  • Founders, the people whose parents aren't in the pedigree, have genotypes from the population, in Hardy–Weinberg proportions. With an allele frequency of q, a person has two copies with a chance of q², one copy with a chance of 2q(1 − q), and none otherwise. Under an X-linked mode, a man has the variant with a chance of q. Someone with only one parent in the pedigree is given an unknown other parent, from the population.
  • Children get one copy of the gene from each parent, and each of a parent's two copies is equally likely to be the one passed on. A son gets his X from his mother, and a daughter gets her father's X. With new mutations, a copy without the variant gains it, on its way to the child, with the chance you give.
  • Being affected depends on the genotype. Someone with the disease genotype is affected with a chance equal to the penetrance, and anyone else with a chance equal to the phenocopy rate. The disease genotype is one copy or more under a dominant mode, and two copies under autosomal recessive; under X-linked recessive, it is a man's one copy, or two copies in a woman.
  • A test result rules genotypes out. Has the variant leaves one copy or two, a carrier leaves one (or, under a dominant mode, one or two), two copies leaves two, and doesn't have it leaves none.

The sums

The chance of everything known of a family is a sum over every combination of everyone's genotypes. The chance of each combination is a product, with a part for each person: the chance of their genotype, given their parents' genotypes (or, for a founder, the population's), times the chance of what is known of them, given their genotype. A family of 20 people has more than 3 billion combinations, but the sum can be done one person at a time. Summing over one person's genotypes leaves a small table for the people they are joined to, and so on through the family. This is the peeling of Elston and Stewart (1971), in a form that works for any pedigree, which is known as variable elimination in a Bayesian network (Lauritzen and Sheehan 2003). It copes with loops, as when cousins have children together, and with any number of generations. Only a pedigree with very many loops is too much for it, and the tab says so.

A person's chance of having each genotype, given everything known, is the sum for that genotype divided by the sum for all of them, which is Bayes' theorem. The chances for a couple's child are worked out in the same way, with the child added to the pedigree.

The Bayesian table

The table under One person is the one in textbooks of risk calculation (Young 2007), but worked out from the whole family. For each of the person's genotypes, it gives:

Prior
the chance of the genotype, from what is known of the rest of the family: of everyone except the person, their identical twins and their descendants;
Conditional
the chance of what is known of the person, their identical twins and their descendants, if the person has the genotype;
Joint
the prior times the conditional;
Posterior
the joint divided by the sum of the joints: the chance of the genotype, given everything known.

The person's own status counts in the conditional, as part of what is known of them. So for the unaffected sister of someone with an autosomal recessive condition, the priors are 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4, for no copies, one and two, and the conditionals 1, 1 and 0, as she is unaffected. Some textbooks start from her chance after that, 2/3 that she is a carrier, as a prior. The posterior is the same either way.

For example, take a woman whose brother has Duchenne muscular dystrophy, an X-linked recessive condition, so that her unaffected mother is a carrier. The woman's prior chance of being a carrier is 1/2. She has two unaffected sons. If she is a carrier, the chance of that is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, and if she isn't, it is 1: these are the conditionals. The joints are 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8 and 1/2 × 1 = 1/2, so her posterior chance of being a carrier is (1/8) / (1/8 + 1/2) = 1/5.

A genotype whose chance is below 1 in a million, both before and after, is left out of the table. When every conditional is tiny, as in a large family of whom much is known, the conditionals and joints are shown as multiples of the largest conditional. This doesn't change the posteriors, which depend only on how the joints compare.

Why a textbook's answer can differ a little

Textbook sums usually leave out the chance that the people who married into the family carry the variant too, and the chance of a new mutation. The Risk tab leaves nothing out, so its answer can differ a little. Take the unaffected daughter of a man who is known to be a carrier of an autosomal recessive condition, in a population in which 1 in 25 unaffected people are carriers. A textbook gives her a chance of 1/2 of being a carrier, and her child with a partner from the population a chance of 1/2 × 1/25 × 1/4 = 1/200 of being affected. But her mother could be a carrier too, so her chance of being one is 50/99, a little more than 1/2, and her child's of being affected is 1 in 198. For the textbook's answer exactly, give the people that it takes not to carry the variant the test result doesn't have it, or make the variant very rare.

How a chance is shown

A chance that is a simple fraction, such as 2/3, 1/4 or 1 in 150, is shown as one. Otherwise, it is shown as about 1 in so many with its percentage, such as about 1 in 12 (8.4%), or, from 1 in 5 upwards, as a percentage, such as about 51%. A chance of less than 1 in a million is shown as that. The saved table has every chance to six significant figures.

How the sums were checked

The sums were checked in two ways. First, the examples of the textbooks come out as the textbooks have them. Among them are 2/3 for the unaffected brother or sister of someone with an autosomal recessive condition, and 1/150 for a child of that brother or sister with a partner from a population in which 1 in 25 people are carriers; 1/5 for the woman with two unaffected sons above, and 1/10 for her next son; 2/3 for the mother of a boy who is the only one in his family with a lethal X-linked recessive condition, when new mutations keep the condition in the population (Haldane's result); 1/6 for the unaffected child of someone with a dominant condition of 80% penetrance; and 1/64 for a child of first cousins whose shared grandfather carries a rare recessive variant. Second, for hundreds of small random pedigrees, under every mode, and with reduced penetrance, phenocopies, new mutations, test results, identical twins and loops, every chance was compared with the same chance worked out by brute force, adding up every way the copies of the gene could have been passed down. They all agree. The checks are in the workbench's repository, in tests/risk.

The GA4GH Pedigree

The Global Alliance for Genomics and Health (GA4GH) is writing a standard for pedigrees. This page explains what the standard looks like so far, how the workbench writes a GA4GH Pedigree and why, and what was wrong with the files our older tools wrote.

The standard so far

The GA4GH Pedigree Standard is still being developed. Its published documentation describes a conceptual model, in which a pedigree is a set of individuals and the relationships between them. Each relationship is named with a term from the Kinship Ontology, KIN. The model isn't a file format of its own. Instead, other standards carry it, in two forms:

  • In Phenopackets. A new message called Pedigree was proposed for the Phenopackets schema in November 2022. In it, the individuals are Phenopackets and the relationships are named by KIN terms. In September 2025 the proposal was closed without being merged, because the Pedigree group hadn't reached a decision yet. It isn't in any release of Phenopackets.
  • In HL7 FHIR. There's a draft implementation guide for this form. Each person is a FHIR Patient, and each relationship is a FamilyMemberHistory.

Neither form has been approved yet. The approved GA4GH format that the workbench writes is Phenopackets version 2. That's the format of the Phenopackets Family, and of the Phenopackets inside the GA4GH Pedigree.

The form the workbench writes

A GA4GH Pedigree from the workbench uses the Phenopackets form of the proposal. It's written in the same way as Phenopackets' JSON, with field names in camelCase:

{
  "id": "FAM1",
  "narrative": "Pedigree drawn in Pedigree Studio: 16 people; proband IV-3. …",
  "date": "2026-10-17",
  "indexPatients": ["IV-3"],
  "individuals": [
    {
      "id": "FAM1-II-3",
      "subject": {"id": "II-3", "vitalStatus": {"status": "ALIVE"}, "sex": "FEMALE"},
      "diseases": [{"term": {"id": "MONDO:0007254", "label": "breast cancer"}}],
      "metaData": {
        "created": "2026-10-17T09:00:00Z", "createdBy": "studio2ga4gh 0.4.0",
        "resources": [{"id": "mondo", "name": "Mondo Disease Ontology", "version": "2026-10-06",
                       "namespacePrefix": "MONDO", "iriPrefix": "http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/MONDO_", …}],
        "phenopacketSchemaVersion": "2.0"
      }
    },
    …
  ],
  "relationships": [
    {"individualId": "I-1", "relation": {"id": "KIN:028", "label": "isBiologicalFatherOf"}, "relativeId": "II-3"},
    …
  ]
}
  • Every individual is a complete Phenopacket version 2, with its own metaData. This means everything about a person – their sex, whether they're alive, their diseases and phenotypes – is held in a structure that is approved and can be checked. Only the wrapper, with its list of individuals and its relationships, comes from the proposal.
  • Each relationship is written once, as one current KIN term, in what the standard calls the reduced form. Each parent is linked to each child. Partners are linked only if they have no children together, or if they're related or separated. All the twins in a set are linked to one of them. Brothers and sisters aren't linked at all, because the parents they share already show that. The page on KIN lists the terms.
  • People are named by their pedigree numbers (II-3) unless you choose otherwise. Phenopackets have no field for a name, so names are never written as such. If you choose to name people by the drawing's labels, though, their ids may be names.
  • A condition without an ontology term is left out, with a note, because a disease or phenotype without an id is invalid.
  • Some things the standard has no field for, such as a carrier or a pregnancy, are noted in the narrative so that they aren't lost.

Why this form

  • The Phenopackets Family that goes with it is already Phenopackets version 2, and the phenopacket toolkit is built around Phenopackets. So there's one model throughout.
  • Because each individual is a Phenopacket, everything recorded about a person can be checked against an approved schema, in the same way that phenopacket-tools, the Phenopackets team's own validator, checks it.
  • The standard's own published examples use this form.

If the GA4GH settles on a different form, only one package, pedigree-ga4gh, will have to change, and the upgrade will rewrite files that are in this form.

One implementation

All of the workbench's tools read and write GA4GH files through one package, pedigree-ga4gh. This means studio2ga4gh, ga4gh2studio and the phenopacket toolkit can no longer disagree about the form. The package's check applies the rules of the Phenopackets schema and of phenopacket-tools, the Phenopackets team's validator, as well as the GA4GH Pedigree's own rules (Check against the standard).

Version 1, in draft

Since June 2026, the Pedigree group has been drafting version 1 of the standard. It hasn't been merged or approved yet, and it may change during review. As drafted, it would give the GA4GH Pedigree a schema of its own:

  • People would be listed with their sex assigned at birth, their dates and their genetic parents. They would be linked to their Phenopackets, rather than being Phenopackets themselves. Whether someone is affected would be recorded in their Phenopacket.
  • A relationship would have separate biological and social KIN terms, a twin type, and a flag for consanguinity.
  • KIN itself would gain 22 terms and retire ten. Three of the terms it would retire are ones the workbench writes: KIN:003, for a biological parent whose sex isn't known, and the two terms for partners who are related.

Until version 1 is approved, the workbench follows the published version. The tools recognise a file in the draft's form and tell you what it is, rather than misreading it, but they can't convert it yet. When version 1 is approved, the change will be in one package, pedigree-ga4gh, and the Phenopackets Family will stay the same. Then the upgrade will rewrite the files the workbench writes now.

Phenopackets

Phenopackets are the GA4GH's approved standard for describing a person's clinical findings – their phenotypes and diseases, and the genetic findings behind them – in a form that computers can read. Version 2 is the current version, and it's the one the workbench writes.

The three messages

A Phenopacket
A record of one person. The subject gives the person's id, sex, vital status and age. The phenotypicFeatures are HPO terms, each observed or excluded, with an onset and severity. The diseases are Mondo terms, and the interpretations are genetic findings. There's also metaData.
A Family
A record of one family. The proband and the relatives with findings are each a Phenopacket. The pedigree lists everyone in the form of a PED file: family, person, father, mother, sex and affected status. consanguinousParents says whether the proband's parents are related, and the Family also has its own metaData.
A Cohort
A set of people who needn't be related. Each of them is a Phenopacket.

What the workbench puts in a Family

  • The proband is the drawing's proband (the index patient), and the relatives are the other people with a condition. A relative with nothing to record is in the pedigree, but doesn't have a Phenopacket.
  • The parents in the pedigree are the genetic ones: biological parents, and egg and sperm donors. Adoptive, foster and step-parents aren't genetic parents, so they aren't written there.
  • consanguinousParents is worked out from the relationships. It's true when the proband's parents are marked consanguineous, or are related as second cousins or closer, which is what KIN means by consanguineous partners.
  • Each Phenopacket has its own metaData, and so does the Family.

Every metaData records when the file was made and what made it (created, createdBy). It also gives the schema version (phenopacketSchemaVersion: 2.0), and a resource for each ontology whose terms are used, with that ontology's release:

"resources": [{"id": "hp", "name": "human phenotype ontology", "url": "http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/hp.owl",
               "version": "2026-09-01", "namespacePrefix": "HP", "iriPrefix": "http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/HP_"}]

Every term's prefix (HP in HP:0000365) must be declared there. This is the rule of phenopacket-tools that files break most often. The release comes from the terms file, the ontology file that was searched, or EBI's service. If none of them gives the release, it's unknown.

The workbench knows the resources of 24 ontologies, from the HPO and Mondo to SNOMED CT and ICD-10-CM. It also keeps any other resource that a file it reads declares in full. If a term comes from an ontology that the workbench can't declare, the term is left out with a warning, rather than making the file invalid.

In JSON

Phenopackets are defined in Protocol Buffers, and written as JSON in the way proto3 writes it. Field names are in camelCase (phenotypicFeatures), and values that are their defaults may be left out. Readers also accept the proto spelling (phenotypic_features). The workbench writes camelCase and reads both.

KIN, the kinship terms

The Kinship Ontology, KIN, is the GA4GH Pedigree Standard's vocabulary of relationships. Each relationship in a GA4GH Pedigree is one KIN term, such as KIN:027 isBiologicalMotherOf.

How a relationship is written

{"individualId": "III-3", "relation": {"id": "KIN:027", "label": "isBiologicalMotherOf"}, "relativeId": "IV-3"}

It reads as a sentence: III-3 is the biological mother of IV-3. The label is taken word for word from KIN. A symmetric relation, such as one between partners, twins or cousins, is written only once, in one direction.

The PED and MPED formats

A PED file is a pedigree written as a table, with one line per person. Linkage and association programs read PED files, and PLINK reads them as .fam files. An MPED file is a PED file with a column for each of several conditions.

The columns

ColumnHolds
1 · familyThe family's id, such as FAM1
2 · personThe person's id, which is unique within the family
3 · father, 4 · motherThe genetic parents' ids, or 0 for a founder (someone with no parents in the file). Adoptive parents and a surrogate aren't genetic parents, so studio2ped writes 0 in their place.
5 · sex1 male, 2 female, 0 unknown
6 · status1 unaffected, 2 affected, 0 unknown
FAM1	I-1	0	0	1	2
FAM1	II-2	I-1	I-2	1	2
FAM1	III-2	II-2	II-1	2	2

The columns are separated by tabs or spaces. Some programs write -9 for an unknown status, but the workbench writes 0, and mpedtools warns about -9. An unknown parent may be -9 too, and mpedtools reads that as 0.

MPED files

An MPED file starts with a line that names its condition columns, and it has one status column for each condition:

# MPED v1	Cystic_fibrosis	Carrier
# FamID	IndID	FatherID	MotherID	Sex	Cystic_fibrosis	Carrier
FAM1	Michael	John	p-jw	1	3	3
FAM1	Amelia	Philip	Ruth	2	2	1

In an MPED file, 3 marks a carrier. A plain PED file has no code for a carrier, so when you take one condition out, a carrier becomes unaffected.

Which tool for what

  • To make a PED file from a drawing, use Drawing → PED or MPED, or go through the GA4GH files. Make a PED file for a linkage program compares the two ways.
  • To draw a PED file, use PED or MPED → drawing.
  • To check PED files, take conditions out of them, or merge and split them, use mpedtools.
  • To read a PED file from a picture of a pedigree, use Picture → PED file and drawing, and to make one up, Simulate a family.
  • To analyse a family, with its loops, kinship, mode of inheritance and candidate variants, use PedNet. It reads PED files, and the first condition of an MPED file.

Ontology terms and terms files

An ontology is a curated list of terms, each with an id, a label, a definition and its place among the others. GA4GH files name conditions with these terms, so that software everywhere reads breast cancer in the same way, whatever it was called in the clinic.

The two ontologies

The Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO)
The HPO covers phenotypes, which are signs and symptoms such as HP:0000365 Hearing impairment. In a Phenopacket they're recorded as phenotypicFeatures, and they can be marked excluded, which means they were looked for and not found.
The Mondo Disease Ontology
Mondo covers diseases, such as MONDO:0007254 breast cancer. It brings them together from other disease vocabularies, including OMIM, Orphanet and ICD. In a Phenopacket they're recorded as diseases.

Use a Mondo term when there's a diagnosis, and HPO terms for what was seen, with or without one.

Ids and releases

  • An id is a CURIE: a prefix, a colon and a number, such as HP:0000365 or MONDO:0007254. Some web pages write HP_0000365, which is the same term, but the workbench writes the colon.
  • New releases of the ontologies come out every month or so. A term may change its label, and now and then a term is made obsolete and replaced. So each GA4GH file records in its metaData the release that its terms came from.

Where a condition gets its term

  1. In the drawing's colour key, as an id in brackets after the name, such as Condition X [MONDO:0009061]. It must be an ontology's id. Hypertension (BP:160/95), for example, is read as a name. The drawing then carries its term wherever it goes. Match conditions to terms can write the ids into a copy of the drawing.
  2. In a terms file, which you give to Drawing → GA4GH or to Fill in missing terms.

The terms file

A terms file is a JSON file that maps each condition's name, as it appears in the colour key, to its term. Match conditions to terms writes it, with each term's label and release:

{
  "Breast cancer": {"id": "MONDO:0007254", "label": "breast cancer", "version": "2026-10-06"},
  "Hearing loss from birth": {"id": "HP:0000365", "label": "Hearing impairment", "version": "2026-09-01"}
}
  • An id on its own will do, as in "Ovarian cancer": "MONDO:0008170". If there's no label, the condition's own name is used, and if there's no release, the release is recorded as unknown.
  • Capital letters don't matter when the names are matched, so one terms file can serve many drawings in a project.
  • The log that Fill in missing terms writes (….log.json) can be used as a terms file too.

Looking terms up

You can search the HPO and Mondo on their own websites, on EBI's Ontology Lookup Service, or here with Match conditions to terms. That tool searches online through EBI, or offline in copies of the ontologies (hp.obo, mondo.obo). Give the conditions ontology terms has advice on choosing a term.

Patient data

Pedigrees are personal data about whole families, including people who haven't consented to anything. Use the workbench as your institution's rules on patient data allow, and follow this advice:

When something goes wrong

These are the common problems, and what to do about each one.

Python doesn't start
The first time you use it, the workbench downloads Python (about 10 MB) from cdn.jsdelivr.net. If your network blocks that site, or you're offline, the tools, the notebook and the programs can't run, although the Studio and the Files tab still work, and so does the Risk tab, except for GA4GH files. Try again on another network. After the first time, the browser usually keeps a copy.
“This GA4GH Pedigree was written by studio2ga4gh 0.3 or earlier …”
The file is in an older form, which the tools no longer read. Upgrade a file from an older tool rewrites it, and you only need to do this once.
A condition is missing from the GA4GH files
It has no ontology term, so it was left out, and the tool mentioned this under its result. Give it a term with Match conditions to terms, then convert the drawing again with the terms file. Check that the name in the terms file is the same as the name in the colour key. Capital letters don't matter, but spelling does.
A colour isn't counted as a condition
The colour key doesn't name it. Open the drawing in the Studio tab, click the key, and type the condition's name next to that colour.
“EBI's Ontology Lookup Service did not answer”
Either the service is down or slow, or your network blocks www.ebi.ac.uk. Try again later, or search copies of the ontologies on this computer instead (2 · Search in).
The PED file has names as ids, or the same id twice
studio2ped names people by their pedigree numbers, unless you choose otherwise in People are named by. The drawing's labels, Pedigree Studio's own ids and the text under the symbols can all be names, so choose pedigree numbers instead. A PED file made by studio2ped 0.1 always used the text under the symbols, which can give names or the same id twice, so make it again (Make a PED file for a linkage program).
The drawing a tool made looks untidy
Press Tidy in the Studio tab, or tick Tidy the drawing when it opens before you run the tool.
The Studio tab shows the wrong drawing, or none
The bar above the drawing shows which file it belongs to. Open… lets you open any of your drawings, and the file button lists the most recent ones.
A tool waits for a long time
A notebook cell may be running. The tools share the notebook's Python, so they wait for the cell to finish. You can stop the cell in the Notebook tab. The first search of a local copy of Mondo also takes a while, because it reads the whole file. The first time a tool of PedNet, PedGen or PedInterp runs, Python downloads it, with what it needs, which is up to about 21 MB, and says so under the form.
“… couldn't be downloaded – check the internet connection”
PedNet, PedGen and PedInterp need NumPy and other packages, which come from cdn.jsdelivr.net, as Python does. If the connection drops, or your network blocks that site, run the tool again once it's back.
Picture → PED file and drawing misses someone, or gives someone the wrong parents
PedInterp needs a clear drawing: dark on white, square to the edges, with each generation in a row of its own and the lines meeting the symbols. A photograph of a page reads worse than a scan or a screenshot. If the pedigree was sketched by hand, even in part, tick Drawn by hand (--hand-drawn in the terminal): PedInterp suggests it when it thinks lines were missed. PedInterp chooses how much to enlarge the picture from the size of its symbols, and a line under the table says how much. If it warns that a line may have been missed, give Enlarge the picture by (or --upscale) a number a little larger or smaller (smaller, if the lines are very thick). If no symbols are found in a page with a small pedigree on it, cut the picture down to the pedigree. In a sketch, a line that stops well short of what it joins, a symbol drawn over a line, a scribble that runs out past a symbol, a line drawn close beside a symbol filled in by scribbling, or a small triangle can be misread, and so can the lines to twins drawn close over their symbols, and two brothers' or sisters' lines drawn leaning towards each other, which are taken for twins': draw it again with a little more room, or correct the drawing afterwards. Half-filled or shaded symbols and dashed lines aren't read yet. Whatever the picture, check what PedInterp read against it: the drawing it makes is laid out as the picture is, so the two are easy to compare. Correct the drawing in the Studio, and make the PED file again from it with Drawing → PED or MPED.
PedNet says that an option needs --family
The kinship table, the graph file and a proband are for one family. If the PED file holds several, give the family's id in Family.
PedNet calls a family that PedGen simulated as recessive something else
Check the form's settings for a recessive condition: four generations or more, cousins who marry, and at least two people affected (Simulate a family). With only the founder affected, nothing shows how a condition is inherited. With a commoner allele than PedGen's default of 0.001, carriers who marry in make a recessive condition look like other modes to PedNet, which takes the allele to be rare. Even so, one family is not always enough: PedNet calls about half of PedGen's four-generation recessive families autosomal recessive, and is uncertain about most of the rest, though it puts autosomal recessive first in five of six. In a family where an affected person marries a carrier, the condition can look dominant.
Simulate a family takes a long time, or says that none of 1,000 families had enough affected
A rare recessive condition seldom turns up twice in a family, so PedGen may have to make many families to find one with as many affected people as you asked for. Let cousins marry (1 is whenever they can), give more generations or children, or ask for fewer affected people.
A workflow can't run, or one of its steps failed
If the workflow can't run, its Run page lists why, such as an input that isn't connected. Open it in the editor, where Checks lists the same things and takes you to each step. If a step failed, open What went wrong under it. It shows the tool's own error, the one you'd see if you ran the tool yourself, and the steps that read its files didn't run. Put right the step's settings in the editor, or choose another input file, and run it again (Workflows).
A workflow's run says stopped
A workflow runs in this page, so closing or reloading the page stops it, and the steps that hadn't run are marked stopped. The files of the steps that had finished are in the run's folder. Run the workflow again.
hponorm or mondonorm stops straight away in the terminal
They ask you questions as they go, and this terminal can't pass your answers to them. Give them the answers in a file, or use Fill in missing terms or Match conditions to terms instead (The Terminal).
My files have gone
Are you using the same browser, on the same computer, and not a private window? The browser keeps the files for this site only. If your browser clears site data when it closes, it deletes them too, so download anything you want to keep.
“The workbench is open in another tab or window too”

It's built from open-source software and open standards, each under its own licence, and THIRD_PARTY.md lists them with their versions.

Standards and data

  • The Phenopackets schema and phenopacket-tools, by the GA4GH. The workbench's checks follow their rules.
  • The Human Phenotype Ontology and the Mondo Disease Ontology, which you search through EBI's Ontology Lookup Service or in copies you download. No copy of either comes with the workbench.
  • The example families come from the lab's pedigree practical, and have been made into Pedigree Studio drawings. The files made from them were written by the packages themselves.
